# Independent audit: one special column or row

## Verdict

I independently audited `round4_one_special_matrix.md` and found no mathematical gap. Together with the independently reviewed scalar ULC theorem, it proves both strong Gram inequalities, and hence the original Gyires inequality, in every order for a doubly stochastic matrix with n−1 identical columns or n−1 identical rows. Equality is exactly at the uniform matrix J_n.

This is an independent mathematical review by another AI agent, not proof-assistant verification or a claim of novelty. The proof's scope is the stated repeated-column/row family; it does not cover arbitrary rank-two matrices.

## 1. Parametrization and permanent identities

After moving the special column to the first position, write the columns as u,v,…,v, where Σu_i=1 and v_i=(1−u_i)/(n−1). Let m=n−1 and a=Σu_i². Then

\[
b=u\cdot v=(1-a)/m,\qquad c=\|v\|^2=(n-2+a)/m^2.
\]

The Gram matrix A^TA has upper-left entry a, first off-diagonal row/column b, and constant lower block c. There are m! permutations in which its first row uses its first column, and m²(m−1)!=m·m! weighted choices in the other case. This gives exactly

\[
\operatorname{per}(A^TA)=m!c^{m-1}(ac+mb^2).
\]

The original permanent is

\[
\operatorname{per}(A)=\frac{m!}{m^m}Q(u),
\qquad
Q(u)=\sum_i u_i\prod_{j\ne i}(1-u_j).
\]

Substitution reduces the second Gram inequality to Q(u)²≥R_n(a), with

\[
R_n(a)=n^{1-n}(n-2+a)^{n-2}(n-1-na+na^2).
\]

All powers and factorials cancel as stated. At a=1/n, R_n(a)=g_n², where g_n=((n−1)/n)^(n−1).

## 2. Lower bounds for Q

The union/product inequality ∏_(j≠i)(1−u_j)≥1−Σ_(j≠i)u_j=u_i gives Q≥Σu_i²=a.

For u_i<1, weighted AM–GM with weights u_i yields

\[
\log\sum_i\frac{u_i}{1-u_i}
\ge-\sum_i u_i\log(1-u_i).
\]

Combining with the product factor in Q proves

\[
\log Q\ge\sum_i(1-u_i)\log(1-u_i).
\]

The second derivative of f(x)=(1−x)log(1−x) is 1/(1−x)≥1. Its strong-convexity inequality around 1/n, summed over i, has zero linear term because Σu_i=1. Thus

\[
Q(u)^2\ge g_n^2\exp(a-1/n).
\]

Zero weights in AM–GM cause no difficulty. The endpoint u_i=1 is handled by continuity; f itself has a continuous extension there. No unproved conjecture about the one-success probability is used.

## 3. The range a≤1/2

Writing d=n−2+a and K=n−1−na+na²,

\[
(\log R_n)'=(n-2)/d+n(2a-1)/K\le1.
\]

Both denominators are positive on the permitted range. The first term is less than1 for n>2, and the second is nonpositive. Integration from 1/n gives R_n(a)≤g_n²exp(a−1/n), strict whenever n>2 and a>1/n. For n=2 this range contains only a=1/2.

## 4. The range a≥1/2

I checked the exact factorization

\[
\left(\log\frac{R_n(a)}{a^2}\right)'
=
\frac{(a-1)\left(n(n-2)a^2+na+2(n-1)(n-2)\right)}
{a(n-2+a)(n-1-na+na^2)}.
\]

It is nonpositive on [1/2,1]. At the left endpoint,

\[
R_n(1/2)=\left(1-\frac3{2n}\right)^{n-2}
\left(\frac34-\frac1n\right).
\]

The proposed binomial-series estimate is valid. With x=3/(2n), k=n−2,

\[
(1-x)^{-k}\ge1+kx+\frac{k(k+1)}2x^2
\ge1+\frac{2k}{n}=3-\frac4n.
\]

For k>0 the second comparison is equivalent to 9(n−1)≥4n, which holds strictly for every integer n≥2. For k=0 both sides are 1 directly. Since (3/4−1/n)=(3−4/n)/4, this proves R_n(1/2)≤1/4, strict for n>2. Monotonicity then gives R_n(a)≤a²≤Q².

For n=2, the endpoint equality is at a=1/2. The displayed logarithmic derivative is strictly negative for 1/2<a<1, so R_n(a)<a² for every a>1/2, including the endpoint by integration. Thus the equality discussion also covers n=2 correctly.

## 5. First Gram inequality, equality, and Gyires

The product polynomial of A has the one-special-variable form covered by the scalar ULC theorem. It is normalized, balanced, and real stable. This proves the first Gram inequality.

The second Gram inequality proved above is strict unless a=1/n. Since Σu_i=1, equality a=1/n forces u_i=1/n for every i, hence A=J_n. This settles equality without needing any unsupported equality claim in the intermediate AM–GM bound.

Permanent Cauchy–Schwarz gives

\[
\operatorname{per}(A^2)
\le\sqrt{\operatorname{per}(AA^T)\operatorname{per}(A^TA)}.
\]

Combining with the two Gram inequalities proves the original Gyires inequality, strict away from J_n. The proof correctly notes that column relabeling need not preserve per(A²): the two Gram conclusions survive relabeling, and the final Cauchy–Schwarz step is applied to the original matrix. Transposition gives the identical-row family.
