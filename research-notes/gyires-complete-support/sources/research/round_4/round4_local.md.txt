# Round four: local deletion geometry for arbitrary balanced stable polynomials

## Status

This note proves a genuine neighborhood result for the proposed deletion inequality, and for the stronger coefficient–Fischer-norm inequality itself. The inputs are arbitrary balanced homogeneous real-stable polynomials, including polynomials that are not products of linear forms. The neighborhood depends on the degree; this is not a proof of the unrestricted conjecture.

The main conclusions are:

1. The stable tangent cone at the uniform power is exactly a cone of negative quadratic contrast perturbations.
2. Every nonzero feasible tangent has a strictly positive first variation of both the average-deletion ratio and the desired coefficient-norm ratio.
3. Compactness upgrades the tangent calculation to a uniform neighborhood theorem, including nondifferentiable approaches to the uniform polynomial.
4. The sharp asymptotic constants in Fischer distance can be computed.
5. The second variation of average deletion is negative along every nonzero straight stable tangent ray when the degree is at least three. Convexity is therefore not the missing global mechanism.
6. A related tangent-cone argument establishes an existence-of-neighborhood statement for the larger Lorentzian class.

The derivations below are mathematical arguments. The small numerical checks at the end verify arithmetic; they are not the proof.

## 1. Conventions

Fix $n\ge2$, put $m=n-1$, and define

\[
R(x)=\frac{x_1+\cdots+x_n}{n},\qquad
p_0(x)=R(x)^n,\qquad
c_n=\frac{n!}{n^n},\qquad
g_n=\left(\frac{n-1}{n}\right)^{n-1}.
\]

All polynomials in the main theorem have real nonnegative coefficients, degree $n$ in $n$ variables, and are normalized and balanced:

\[
p(\mathbf1)=1,\qquad
\partial_ip(\mathbf1)=1\quad(1\le i\le n).
\tag{1}
\]

The squared Fischer norm and its associated inner product are

\[
N(p)=\sum_\alpha\alpha!p_\alpha^2,\qquad
\langle p,q\rangle_F=\sum_\alpha\alpha!p_\alpha q_\alpha.
\]

Let $a(p)=[x_1\cdots x_n]p$. The target norm inequality is

\[
a(p)^2\ge c_nN(p).
\tag{2}
\]

For a matrix product $p_A=\prod_i(Ax)_i$, this is exactly the strong permanent inequality, since $a(p_A)=\operatorname{per}A$ and $N(p_A)=\operatorname{per}(AA^T)$.

For $q_j=[x_j]p$, choose a positive capacity-balancing diagonal $D_j$ and set

\[
H_j(p)=\frac{N(q_j(D_jx))}{(\det D_j)^2}.
\]

Near $p_0$, all coefficients are positive, balancing exists uniquely up to a common scalar, and the value is unambiguous. Define

\[
\mathcal D_{\rm av}(p)=\frac{\frac1n\sum_jH_j(p)}{g_nN(p)},
\qquad
\mathcal D_{\max}(p)=\frac{\max_jH_j(p)}{g_nN(p)},
\qquad
\mathcal S(p)=\frac{a(p)^2}{c_nN(p)}.
\tag{3}
\]

All three ratios equal one at $p_0$.

There is an exact orthogonality identity:

\[
\langle p_0,h\rangle_F=c_nh(\mathbf1).
\]

Consequently, for every normalized $p$,

\[
N(p)=c_n+N(p-p_0).
\tag{4}
\]

It is convenient to measure distance by

\[
d_F(p,p_0)=\sqrt{\frac{N(p-p_0)}{c_n}}
          =\sqrt{\frac{N(p)}{c_n}-1}.
\tag{5}
\]

## 2. Capacity balancing is analytic near the uniform deletion

The uniform deletion is

\[
q_{0,j}(x)=g_n\left(\frac{\sum_{i\ne j}x_i}{m}\right)^m.
\]

Use logarithmic diagonal coordinates $y$ with $\sum_i y_i=0$. The capacity objective is

\[
\Psi_q(y)=\log q(e^y)-\sum_i y_i.
\]

Its gradient is the mean exponent minus $\mathbf1$, and its Hessian is the covariance matrix of the tilted coefficient distribution. At the uniform deletion, that distribution is multinomial with $m$ trials and probabilities $1/m$, so

\[
\nabla^2\Psi_{q_0}(0)=I-\frac{\mathbf1\mathbf1^T}{m}.
\]

This is the identity on the gauge subspace $\mathbf1^\perp$. The implicit-function theorem therefore makes the balancing logarithms analytic functions of the coefficients of $q$ near $q_0$. Positivity of all coefficients gives coercivity and makes this critical point the unique global capacity minimizer on the gauge.

This justifies differentiating $H_j$ and using ordinary Taylor expansions of the ratios in (3).

## 3. First and second variations without assuming stability

Consider a normalized balanced coefficient path

\[
p_t=p_0+t h+t^2k+o(t^2).
\]

Thus $h(\mathbf1)=k(\mathbf1)=0$ and $\nabla h(\mathbf1)=\nabla k(\mathbf1)=0$. Write

\[
s_j=[x_j]h,\qquad
a_j=s_j(\mathbf1),\qquad
v_j=\nabla s_j(\mathbf1)-a_j\mathbf1,\qquad
b_j=([x_j]k)(\mathbf1).
\tag{6}
\]

Here the gradients have $m$ coordinates. Homogeneity gives $\sum_i(v_j)_i=0$.

The first balancing displacement is

\[
\log D_j(t)=-\frac{t}{g_n}v_j+O(t^2)
\tag{7}
\]

in the zero-sum gauge.

The resulting norm expansion is

\[
H_j(p_t)=g_nc_n+2c_na_jt
+\left(N(s_j)-2c_m\|v_j\|^2+2c_nb_j\right)t^2+o(t^2).
\tag{8}
\]

Also, by (4),

\[
N(p_t)=c_n+N(h)t^2+o(t^2).
\tag{9}
\]

### Derivation of (8)

Let $L_d=\sum_i d_i x_i\partial_i$, with $d=-v_j/g_n$. The first perturbation after diagonal scaling is $s_j+L_dq_0$. The uniform Fischer functional gives

\[
\langle q_0,f\rangle_F=c_nf(\mathbf1).
\]

The first-order terms involving the balancing displacement vanish because $\sum_i d_i=0$. At second order,

\[
N(s_j+L_dq_0)=N(s_j)-c_m\|v_j\|^2.
\]

The cross term with the second-order change of the scaled polynomial contributes another $-c_m\|v_j\|^2+2c_nb_j$. Terms involving the unknown second-order balancing displacement vanish against $q_0$ because that displacement also has zero sum. This yields (8).

For a straight coefficient path, $k=0$, the quadratic coefficient of
$\frac1n\sum_jH_j-g_nN(p)$ is therefore exactly

\[
\mathcal Q(h)=
\frac1n\sum_jN([x_j]h)
-\frac{2c_m}{n}\sum_j\|v_j\|^2
-g_nN(h).
\tag{10}
\]

Equivalently, if $k_1(\alpha)$ counts coordinates of $\alpha$ equal to one,

\[
\mathcal Q(h)=
\sum_\alpha\left(\frac{k_1(\alpha)}n-g_n\right)\alpha!h_\alpha^2
-\frac{2c_m}{n}\sum_j\|v_j\|^2.
\tag{11}
\]

For arbitrary directions, the first directional derivative of $\mathcal D_{\max}$ is

\[
\frac{2}{g_n}\max_j a_j.
\]

Its second coefficient is the largest individual second coefficient among the indices attaining that first-order maximum. Thus the nonsmooth maximum is fully accounted for; it must not be differentiated as an arbitrary fixed index.

## 4. Exact stable tangent cone at the uniform power

Let $p_t$ be a one-sided differentiable path of normalized balanced real-stable polynomials with $p_t=p_0+t h+o(t)$.

**Tangent theorem.** There is a unique symmetric matrix $B$ such that

\[
h(x)=-R(x)^{n-2}x^TBx,\qquad
B\succeq0,\qquad B\mathbf1=0.
\tag{12}
\]

Conversely, every such $B$ is a realizable stable tangent.

### Necessity, including arbitrary differentiable paths

Fix a real contrast vector $x$ with $\mathbf1^Tx=0$. Real stability implies that

\[
f_t(z)=p_t(z\mathbf1+x)
\]

is a monic real-rooted polynomial. Its leading coefficient is $p_t(\mathbf1)=1$. Its coefficient of $z^{n-1}$ is $\nabla p_t(\mathbf1)\cdot x=0$. Write its real roots as $\rho_1(t),\ldots,\rho_n(t)$. They satisfy

\[
\sum_i\rho_i(t)=0,\qquad
[z^{n-2}]f_t=-\frac12\sum_i\rho_i(t)^2.
\]

The second coefficient is $O(t)$, because the corresponding coefficient of $f_0(z)=z^n$ vanishes. Therefore

\[
\sum_i\rho_i(t)^2=O(t),\qquad
|\rho_i(t)|=O(\sqrt t).
\]

Every elementary symmetric function of order $r\ge3$ is consequently $O(t^{r/2})=o(t)$. Dividing by $t$ shows that the first variation has no contrast grades three or higher. Its grade-two part is nonpositive for every real contrast $x$. Normalization removes grade zero, and balance removes grade one. These facts are exactly (12).

No analytic dependence of individual roots is assumed, and no higher-order coefficient is silently discarded. The root-variance bound is what proves it is $o(t)$.

### Realizability and nonproduct examples

Given $B\succeq0$ with $B\mathbf1=0$, put

\[
p_t(x)=R(x)^{n-2}\left(R(x)^2-tx^TBx\right).
\tag{13}
\]

For sufficiently small $t\ge0$, the quadratic in parentheses has strictly positive coefficients. Its quadratic-form matrix is

\[
\frac{\mathbf1\mathbf1^T}{n^2}-tB.
\]

It has one positive eigenvalue along $\mathbf1$ and only nonpositive eigenvalues on $\mathbf1^\perp$. It is positive on every strictly positive real vector. To check stability directly, suppose it vanished at $x+iy$ with $y>0$. Its real and imaginary parts would give $Q(x)=Q(y)>0$ and the bilinear orthogonality $Q(x,y)=0$. The orthogonal complement of a positive vector for a form with only one positive direction is negative semidefinite, a contradiction. Thus the quadratic, and hence (13), is real stable.

Normalization and balance follow from $B\mathbf1=0$. If $B$ has rank at least two, the quadratic generally has rank at least three and cannot factor into two real linear forms. These directions therefore include genuinely nonproduct stable perturbations.

## 5. Stable first variations and exact second coefficient

For (12), set

\[
\kappa_n=\left(\frac{n-1}{n}\right)^{n-3}.
\]

Specializing all variables except $x_j$ to one gives

\[
h(1,\ldots,z,\ldots,1)
=-B_{jj}\left(\frac{n-1+z}{n}\right)^{n-2}(z-1)^2.
\]

Consequently,

\[
a_j=\kappa_nB_{jj}.
\]

Equations (8)–(9) now yield

\[
\mathcal D_{\rm av}(p_t)
=1+\frac{2n}{(n-1)^2}\operatorname{tr}B\,t+o(t),
\tag{14}
\]

\[
\mathcal D_{\max}(p_t)
=1+2\left(\frac{n}{n-1}\right)^2\max_jB_{jj}\,t+o(t).
\tag{15}
\]

Every nonzero positive-semidefinite $B$ has positive trace and at least one positive diagonal entry. Thus every nonzero stable tangent points strictly into the desired deletion inequality.

For the straight realizations (13), the complete quadratic coefficient of $\mathcal D_{\rm av}$ is

\[
\boxed{
Q_n(B)=
\frac{n^2(3n^2-5n+4)}{(n-1)^5}\sum_jB_{jj}^2
-\frac{2n(2n-1)}{(n-1)^3}\operatorname{tr}(B^2).
}
\tag{16}
\]

The formula follows by orthogonally decomposing each deletion perturbation into contrast grades zero, one, and two. If $r_j$ is the projection of the off-diagonal part of column $j$ onto $\mathbf1_m^\perp$, and $B_j$ is the corresponding compression of $B$ to that contrast subspace, the individual quadratic coefficient before averaging is

\[
\left(\frac{n}{m}\right)^4
\left(B_{jj}^2-\frac4{n^2}\|r_j\|^2
+\frac{2m(n-2)}{n^2}\operatorname{tr}(B_j^2)\right)
-\frac{2n}{m}\operatorname{tr}(B^2).
\tag{17}
\]

The identities used to average it are

\[
\sum_j\|r_j\|^2
=\operatorname{tr}(B^2)-\frac n{n-1}\sum_jB_{jj}^2,
\]

\[
\sum_j\operatorname{tr}(B_j^2)
=\frac{n(n-3)}{n-1}\operatorname{tr}(B^2)
+\frac{n^2}{(n-1)^2}\sum_jB_{jj}^2.
\]

For $n\ge3$, (16) is strictly negative whenever $B\ne0$. Indeed, $B\mathbf1=0$ and Cauchy–Schwarz give

\[
\sum_jB_{jj}^2\le\frac{n-1}{n}\operatorname{tr}(B^2),
\]

and therefore

\[
Q_n(B)\le
-\frac{n(n-2)(n+1)}{(n-1)^4}\operatorname{tr}(B^2)<0.
\tag{18}
\]

This negative second variation does not invalidate the local inequality: the positive first variation dominates it. It does rule out a proof based on convexity of this ratio along even these simple feasible rays.

## 6. A genuine neighborhood theorem with sharp asymptotic constants

Orthogonal invariance of the Fischer norm, and $B\mathbf1=0$, give

\[
N(h)=\frac{2(n-2)!}{n^{n-2}}\operatorname{tr}(B^2)
=c_n\frac{2n}{n-1}\operatorname{tr}(B^2).
\tag{19}
\]

Combining (14) with (19),

\[
\frac{\mathcal D_{\rm av}(p_t)-1}{d_F(p_t,p_0)}
\longrightarrow
\frac{\sqrt{2n}}{(n-1)^{3/2}}
\frac{\operatorname{tr}B}{\sqrt{\operatorname{tr}(B^2)}}.
\tag{20}
\]

For $B\succeq0$, the final factor is at least one.

The same estimate holds uniformly over all stable approaches, not just fixed differentiable paths. To see this, take any sequence $p_\ell\to p_0$ of distinct normalized balanced stable polynomials and set

\[
t_\ell=\|p_\ell-p_0\|_F,\qquad
h_\ell=\frac{p_\ell-p_0}{t_\ell}.
\]

The unit sphere is compact in this finite-dimensional coefficient space. Every convergent subsequence of $h_\ell$ has a nonzero limit $h$. The root-variance argument in Section 4 applies with the coefficient difference $O(t_\ell)$, and forces this limit to have form (12). Analyticity of the deletion functional then identifies its first variation with (14). Since the lower bound in (20) is uniform over the unit tangent cone,

\[
\liminf_{\substack{p\to p_0\\
p\ne p_0,\ p\ {\rm balanced,\ stable}}}
\frac{\mathcal D_{\rm av}(p)-1}{d_F(p,p_0)}
\ge\frac{\sqrt{2n}}{(n-1)^{3/2}}.
\tag{21}
\]

Rank-one matrices $B=vv^T$ with $v\perp\mathbf1$ realize equality in the asymptotic bound. Thus this constant is sharp.

In particular, there exists $\delta_n>0$ such that

\[
0<d_F(p,p_0)<\delta_n
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\mathcal D_{\rm av}(p)>1.
\tag{22}
\]

The maximum-deletion version follows because $\mathcal D_{\max}\ge\mathcal D_{\rm av}$. This proves that the proposed deletion lemma cannot fail infinitesimally at uniform, even along nonsmooth sequences.

## 7. Direct local proof of the desired norm inequality

The central coefficient of a stable tangent is

\[
a(h)=\frac{(n-2)!}{n^{n-2}}\operatorname{tr}B
=c_n\frac n{n-1}\operatorname{tr}B.
\tag{23}
\]

The identity follows by observing that square terms in $x^TBx$ cannot contribute to the central monomial, while
$\sum_{i\ne j}B_{ij}=-\operatorname{tr}B$.

Therefore

\[
\mathcal S(p_t)
=1+\frac{2n}{n-1}\operatorname{tr}B\,t+o(t).
\tag{24}
\]

The same compactness argument gives the sharp estimate

\[
\liminf_{\substack{p\to p_0\\
p\ne p_0,\ p\ {\rm balanced,\ stable}}}
\frac{\mathcal S(p)-1}{d_F(p,p_0)}
\ge\sqrt{\frac{2n}{n-1}}.
\tag{25}
\]

Again rank-one $B$ attains the asymptotic constant. Consequently the strong coefficient-norm inequality (2) holds strictly in a neighborhood of the uniform power for every nonuniform normalized balanced stable polynomial. This conclusion is independent of the unproved global deletion lemma.

For straight stable tangent rays, the second coefficient of $\mathcal S$ is

\[
\frac{n^2}{(n-1)^2}(\operatorname{tr}B)^2
-\frac{2n}{n-1}\operatorname{tr}(B^2).
\]

## 8. A global variance-deficit energy and what it does not prove

For any normalized balanced stable polynomial, let $P$ be orthogonal projection onto $\mathbf1^\perp$ and set

\[
B(p)=-\frac12P\nabla^2p(\mathbf1)P.
\]

The real-root variance argument gives $B(p)\succeq0$. If the coefficient distribution of $p$ is denoted by $\alpha$, then

\[
E(p):=2\operatorname{tr}B(p)
=n-1-\operatorname{tr}\operatorname{Cov}(\alpha)\ge0.
\tag{26}
\]

Moreover, $E(p)=0$ forces every contrast restriction $p(z\mathbf1+x)$ to have all its roots zero, and hence forces $p=p_0$.

For a doubly stochastic matrix product,

\[
B(p_A)=\frac12(A-J_n)^T(A-J_n),\qquad
E(p_A)=\|A-J_n\|_F^2.
\]

Under uniform mixing,

\[
p_s(x)=p\bigl((J_n+s(I-J_n))x\bigr),\qquad 0\le s\le1,
\]

the exact relation is $E(p_s)=s^2E(p)$.

There is also a contrast-degree expansion

\[
p(x)=R^n-R^{n-2}x^TB(p)x+\sum_{r=3}^nR^{n-r}f_r(x),
\]

where each $f_r$ depends only on the contrast variables. For a unit contrast $x$, elementary symmetric-function bounds on the real roots give

\[
|f_r(x)|\le
\binom nr\left(\frac{2x^TB(p)x}{n}\right)^{r/2}.
\tag{27}
\]

Finite-dimensional norm equivalence therefore bounds the higher grades by $O_n(E^{r/2})$. It follows directly that, as $E\to0$,

\[
\mathcal D_{\rm av}(p)=1+\frac{n}{(n-1)^2}E(p)+O_n(E(p)^{3/2}),
\]

\[
\mathcal S(p)=1+\frac{n}{n-1}E(p)+O_n(E(p)^{3/2}).
\tag{28}
\]

This supplies another proof of the neighborhood statements. It does not control the signs of all higher-order contributions when $E$ is large.

### The deletion ratio is not globally monotone under uniform mixing

Take $A=(I+P_3)/2$, and $A_s=J_3+s(A-J_3)$. Its product polynomial has central coefficient

\[
b(s)=\frac29+\frac{s^2}{12}-\frac{s^3}{18}
\]

and all six mixed coefficients of type $(2,1,0)$ equal

\[
a(s)=\frac19+\frac{s^3}{72}.
\]

Each deleted quadratic is already balanced, so

\[
H(s)=b(s)^2+4a(s)^2.
\]

At $s=1$,

\[
H=\frac18,\quad H'=\frac1{24},\quad
N=\frac14,\quad N'=\frac18.
\]

Consequently,

\[
\left.\frac d{ds}\frac{H(s)}{g_3N(s)}\right|_{s=1}
=-\frac3{16}.
\tag{29}
\]

The energy $E$ increases along this ray while the deletion ratio turns down near its endpoint. The energy is useful for local control, but monotonicity of the ratio cannot be inferred from it.

## 9. The Lorentzian tangent cone also has a strict first-order margin

For background, the full-support characterization of Lorentzian polynomials is used here: all derivatives of order $n-2$ have Hessians with at most one positive eigenvalue. Full monomial support is automatically M-convex. The reference is Brändén and Huh, “Lorentzian polynomials,” Annals of Mathematics 192 (2020), 821–891; see the primary paper at https://annals.math.princeton.edu/wp-content/uploads/annals-v192-n3-p04-s.pdf. Its binary example identifies Lorentzian forms with log-concavity of binomially normalized coefficients.

At $p_0$, every Hessian of an order-$n-2$ derivative equals $c_n\mathbf1\mathbf1^T$. Therefore a normalized balanced Lorentzian tangent $h$ satisfies

\[
P\left(\nabla^2\partial^\beta h\right)P\preceq0
\qquad (|\beta|=n-2).
\tag{30}
\]

Conversely, these inequalities characterize the tangent cone at full support. In the block decomposition along $\mathbf1$ and $\mathbf1^\perp$, the Schur complement of the surviving positive entry is
$tC-t^2bb^T/(a_0+ta)$, which is negative semidefinite whenever $C\preceq0$. Thus $p_0+t h$ has the requisite derivative-Hessian signatures for small positive $t$.

This cone is larger than the stable tangent cone: it may contain higher contrast grades.

### Why the deletion first variation is still positive

Fix $j$, and write the one-coordinate marginal variation as

\[
h(1,\ldots,z,\ldots,1)=\sum_{r=0}^nh_{j,r}z^r.
\]

Let

\[
b_r=\binom nr\frac{(n-1)^{n-r}}{n^n},\qquad
z_r=\frac{h_{j,r}}{b_r}.
\]

The derivative-Hessian inequalities (30), applied to the binary marginal and its mixed derivatives, give

\[
2z_r-z_{r-1}-z_{r+1}\ge0.
\]

One can obtain this directly without assuming an unproved closure rule: the relevant marginal derivative is a positive sum of the matrices in (30), restricted to the binary contrast direction $(n-1,-1)$.

Thus $z_r$ is a concave sequence. For $K\sim\operatorname{Bin}(n,1/n)$, normalization and balance give

\[
\mathbb E z_K=0,\qquad \mathbb E(Kz_K)=0,\qquad \mathbb EK=1.
\]

Jensen's inequality gives $z_1\ge0$, hence $a_j=h_{j,1}=g_nz_1\ge0$.

If $a_j=0$, equality in Jensen and full support of $K$ force $z_r$ to be affine in $r$. The two moment constraints then force it to be identically zero.

Suppose all $a_j$ vanish. Every marginal variation vanishes, so all diagonal entries of $\nabla^2h(\mathbf1)$ are zero. The positive weighted sum of the matrices in (30) is the projected Hessian of $h$ at $\mathbf1$. Balance gives $\nabla^2h(\mathbf1)\mathbf1=0$, so this Hessian is negative semidefinite with zero trace and must vanish. Hence every projected matrix in (30) vanishes. All second directional derivatives in contrast directions then vanish identically, and

\[
h=aR^n+R^{n-1}L
\]

for a contrast linear form $L$. Normalization and balance force $a=L=0$.

We conclude that

\[
h\ne0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \sum_j a_j>0
\tag{31}
\]

on the normalized balanced Lorentzian tangent cone.

Compactness of its intersection with the unit sphere now proves a strict average-deletion neighborhood for normalized balanced Lorentzian polynomials as well. No sharp constant for this larger cone is asserted here.

The local strong norm inequality extends to this larger class by induction on the degree: deletion, specialization, and positive diagonal scaling preserve the derivative-Hessian characterization and full support; normalized balanced deletions converge to the lower-degree uniform power. Use the lower-degree local norm theorem together with the strict local average-deletion theorem. Degree two is the elementary balanced binary-quadratic inequality. This argument is local only; it makes no claim that the deletion inequality is globally true for Lorentzian polynomials.

## 10. Fischer operators identify the missing global issue

Embed $q_j$ back into the degree-$n$ space as $x_jq_j$. This is precisely the orthogonal projection of $p$ onto the monomials with exponent $\alpha_j=1$. Therefore

\[
\sum_jN(q_j)=
\sum_\alpha k_1(\alpha)\alpha!p_\alpha^2.
\tag{32}
\]

By contrast, ordinary differentiation satisfies the exact identity

\[
\sum_jN(\partial_jp)=nN(p).
\tag{33}
\]

The difference is the projection onto occupancy exactly one. The operator in (32) has eigenvalues $k_1(\alpha)$, including zero, so it has no positive spectral lower bound on the full homogeneous polynomial space. Capacity balancing is nonlinear and is chosen by the coefficient-sum capacity, rather than by minimizing the Fischer norm.

An explicit nearby example shows that positivity and balance alone cannot repair this operator obstruction. In degree three let

\[
V=(x_1-x_2)(x_2-x_3)(x_3-x_1),\qquad
p_\varepsilon=\left(\frac{x_1+x_2+x_3}{3}\right)^3+\varepsilon V.
\]

For $0<|\varepsilon|<1/9$, all coefficients remain positive, and normalization and balance remain exact. But

\[
N(p_\varepsilon)=\frac29+12\varepsilon^2,
\qquad
H_j(p_\varepsilon)=\frac8{81}-4\varepsilon^2
\quad\text{for all }j.
\]

Hence

\[
\frac13\sum_jH_j-g_3N(p_\varepsilon)
=-\frac{28}{3}\varepsilon^2<0.
\tag{34}
\]

The central coefficient is unchanged, so (2) also fails. These polynomials are not stable: for a contrast $x$ with $V(x)\ne0$, the restriction is $z^3+\varepsilon V(x)$, which has a nonreal conjugate pair of roots. This example makes the role of the stable or Lorentzian tangent restrictions explicit.

## 11. Boundary subtraction is valid, but does not connect the boundary to the local theorem

There is a valid all-order reduction for balanced Lorentzian polynomials. Let $S=\sum_i x_i$ and let

\[
\mu=\min_i[x_i^n]p.
\]

The normalized coefficients

\[
\bar p_\alpha=\frac{p_\alpha}{\binom n\alpha}
\]

are log-concave along every coordinate-transfer line. This follows directly from the $2\times2$ principal minors of the quadratic derivative Hessians. Along such a line an interior value is at least the smaller endpoint value. Repeatedly concentrating two occupied coordinates into one therefore gives

\[
\bar p_\alpha\ge\mu\quad\text{for every }\alpha.
\tag{35}
\]

For $0\le t<\mu$, the polynomial $p-tS^n$ has positive coefficients. Its order-$n-2$ derivative Hessians are those of $p$ minus $tn!\mathbf1\mathbf1^T$. Subtracting a positive-semidefinite matrix cannot increase the number of positive eigenvalues. Thus $p-tS^n$ remains Lorentzian. Closure gives the same conclusion at $t=\mu$.

Balance is preserved because $S^n$ is balanced. Unless $p=p_0$ after normalization, the residual

\[
q=\frac{p-\mu S^n}{1-\mu n^n}
\]

is a normalized balanced Lorentzian polynomial with at least one zero pure coefficient.

The target condition is the positive branch of a second-order cone:

\[
a(p)\ge\sqrt{c_n}\,\|p\|_F.
\]

It is convex, and $S^n$ lies on its boundary. Hence proving the norm inequality for all such pure-coefficient boundary polynomials would prove it for all balanced Lorentzian polynomials. This subtraction need not preserve real stability, so it is a reduction to the broader Lorentzian problem.

There is a stronger ratio statement. For any normalized nonnegative $q$, write

\[
p_\lambda=(1-\lambda)p_0+\lambda q,\quad
u=\frac{a(q)}{c_n}-1,\quad
v=\frac{N(q)}{c_n}-1\ge0.
\]

Then

\[
\mathcal S(p_\lambda)=\frac{(1+\lambda u)^2}{1+\lambda^2v}.
\tag{36}
\]

Its only possible interior critical point is a maximum. Therefore

\[
\mathcal S(p_\lambda)\ge\min\{1,\mathcal S(q)\}.
\tag{37}
\]

Any counterexample can consequently be moved to the pure-coefficient boundary without improving its ratio. A counterexample that is an interior local minimum cannot occur, because the uniform-subtraction direction strictly decreases the ratio whenever it is below one.

This does not combine with the local theorem to finish the proof. The balanced Lorentzian class is not star-shaped about $p_0$. For example,

\[
q(x)=\frac14x_1(x_2+x_3)^2
\]

is normalized, balanced, and real stable. For every $0<\lambda<1$, the Hessian of
$\partial_1((1-\lambda)p_0+\lambda q)$ is a positive sum of two nonparallel rank-one positive-semidefinite matrices. It has two positive eigenvalues, so the intermediate polynomial is not Lorentzian. Thus even the straight segment from a valid boundary input to uniform can leave the admissible class immediately.

The reduction identifies the remaining possible extremizers as boundary points; it does not classify those boundary points or establish their norm inequality.

## 12. Verification and remaining gap

The script round4_local_check.py independently constructs coefficient arrays and balances deletions numerically. For rank-one and full-rank projection directions in degrees two through six, it checks (19) and the first and second coefficients in (14) and (16). All checks passed; the output is round4_local_check.json. The full-rank directions are nonproduct stable perturbations for degree at least three.

The unrestricted problem remains open in this work. The new local result rules out an infinitesimal obstruction at uniform, proves a uniform neighborhood statement, and identifies an exact first- and second-order structure. What is still missing is a global inequality controlling the higher contrast grades or the nonlinear capacity-balanced deletion norm away from that neighborhood.
