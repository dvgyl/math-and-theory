# Why scalar localization does not directly extend to the Lorentzian cone

## Main result

The scalar proof uses two-sided linear perturbations of the coefficient sequence. At an active binary log-concavity equality, its first-order condition automatically makes the second-order term favorable.

For multivariate Lorentzian polynomials this implication fails. The missing condition concerns how a perturbation couples the kernel of a quadratic derivative Hessian to its negative eigenspace. There is an exact balanced three-variable product polynomial for which the first-order conditions leave a nonzero direction, but that direction creates a second positive Hessian eigenvalue at second order. Consequently, a direct localization principle asserting that every non-binary-lift input admits a nonzero two-sided feasible perturbation is false.

This does not rule out a more elaborate localization theorem that also compares the norms of other extremal shapes. It does show that the scalar argument alone does not force global maximizers to be binary lifts.

## 1. Exact local inertia conditions

Let H be a symmetric quadratic derivative Hessian with one positive eigenvalue, r negative eigenvalues, and k zero eigenvalues. In an orthonormal decomposition into its range and kernel, write

\[
H=\begin{pmatrix}D&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
K=\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\B^T&C\end{pmatrix},
\]

where D is invertible of inertia (1,r). A coefficient perturbation p±εh produces H±εK because quadratic derivative Hessians depend linearly on the coefficients.

For both signs to preserve the condition of at most one positive eigenvalue for all sufficiently small ε, it is necessary that

\[
C=0.
\tag{1}
\]

Indeed any nonzero eigenvalue of C becomes positive for one of the two signs, in addition to the positive eigenvalue inherited from D.

When C=0, the Schur complement gives the exact condition

\[
H+\varepsilon K\text{ has at most one positive eigenvalue}
\iff
B^T(D+\varepsilon A)^{-1}B\succeq0
\tag{2}
\]

for sufficiently small nonzero ε. The nonzero range block retains inertia (1,r), and the kernel Schur complement is

\[
-\varepsilon^2B^T(D+\varepsilon A)^{-1}B.
\]

Thus first-order flatness (1) must be supplemented at least by

\[
B^TD^{-1}B\succeq0.
\tag{3}
\]

A useful sufficient condition is (3) together with ker(B^TD^(-1)B)=ker B: positivity then persists on the fixed complementary subspace for both signs. The simpler linear condition K(ker H)=0 is also sufficient. In an isotropic case where the two kernels differ, higher-order terms in (2) still need examination.

For a rank-one positive semidefinite H, its range block D is positive, so (3) is automatic and its kernel equals ker B. This is the binary active-equality situation used in the scalar proof. Once D also has a negative direction, the sign in (3) can be wrong.

For a polynomial of degree n, each quadratic derivative H_β, |β|=n−2, supplies such conditions. If Z_β spans ker H_β, first-order flatness means

\[
Z_\beta^TK_\beta Z_\beta=0.
\]

These are linear equations on the coefficient perturbation. The extra conditions (3) are quadratic and need not follow from a favorable dimension count.

## 2. A balanced non-binary polynomial with no two-sided feasible direction

Consider

\[
p_0(x,y,z)=(x+y)(y+z)(z+x)
=x^2y+x^2z+xy^2+xz^2+y^2z+yz^2+2xyz.
\tag{4}
\]

It is a product polynomial of a doubly stochastic matrix after division by 8:

\[
\frac{p_0}{8}=p_A,
\qquad
A=\frac12\begin{pmatrix}1&1&0\\0&1&1\\1&0&1\end{pmatrix}.
\]

It is real stable and Lorentzian. Also p_0(1)=8 and every first derivative at 1 is 8, so it is balanced before or after scalar normalization. Its central coefficient is2.

Suppose p_0±εh is coefficientwise nonnegative, balanced, has the same central coefficient, and is Lorentzian for both signs of sufficiently small ε. The three zero pure-cube coefficients force h to have no pure cubes. The central coefficient of h is zero. Write its remaining coefficients as

\[
h=A x^2y+B x^2z+Cxy^2+Dxz^2+Ey^2z+Fyz^2.
\]

The derivative Hessians of p_0 have kernels

\[
\ker H_x=\operatorname{span}(0,1,-1),\quad
\ker H_y=\operatorname{span}(1,0,-1),\quad
\ker H_z=\operatorname{span}(1,-1,0).
\]

The necessary first-order conditions (1) give

\[
C+D=0,\qquad A+F=0,\qquad B+E=0.
\tag{5}
\]

Balance of h means ∂_i h(1)=h(1). Substituting (5), two independent balance equations become A+B=0 and C−B=0. Hence

\[
(A,B,C,D,E,F)=a(1,-1,-1,1,1,-1),
\]

so every possible nonzero first-order direction is a multiple of

\[
V=(x-y)(x-z)(y-z).
\tag{6}
\]

But for p_t=p_0+tV,

\[
H_x(t)=\operatorname{Hess}(\partial_xp_t)
=\begin{pmatrix}
0&2(1+t)&2(1-t)\\
2(1+t)&2(1-t)&2\\
2(1-t)&2&2(1+t)
\end{pmatrix}.
\tag{7}
\]

Its determinant and trace are

\[
\det H_x(t)=-64t^2,\qquad\operatorname{tr}H_x(t)=4.
\]

For every t≠0 it has two positive eigenvalues and one negative eigenvalue. Therefore no nonzero direction survives for both signs. The same determinant identity holds for the other two derivative Hessians.

This example is a two-sided-rigid point of the balanced, central-normalized Lorentzian set on its coefficient support, for linear coefficient perturbations. It is not a one-special-variable lift: the term yz remains visible independently of y+z in (4), and symmetry gives the same obstruction for every choice of special variable.

### The second-order failure in integer coordinates

Use the basis e_1, e_2+e_3, e_2−e_3. The congruent matrix in (7) is

\[
\begin{pmatrix}
0&4&4t\\
4&8&-4t\\
4t&-4t&0
\end{pmatrix}.
\]

Its range block is D=((0,4),(4,8)), its first-order kernel block is zero, and the coupling vector is b=(4,−4). Direct calculation gives

\[
b^TD^{-1}b=-16.
\]

Consequently the kernel Schur complement is +16t². This exhibits precisely the negative-range coupling missing from the scalar perturbation argument.

## 3. Even all binary coefficient inequalities and marginals are insufficient

For |t|<1, p_t has nonnegative coefficients and remains balanced with central coefficient 2. Every coordinate marginal is unchanged:

\[
p_t(x,1,1)=2+4x+2x^2,
\]

and similarly for y and z.

Moreover all 2×2 principal minors of every derivative Hessian are among

\[
-4(1+t)^2,\qquad-4(1-t)^2,\qquad-4t^2.
\]

Thus every binary coefficient-line log-concavity condition remains valid. The obstruction is the full Hessian signature, not a failure of those one-dimensional inequalities.

The norm is exactly

\[
N(p_t)=16+12t^2.
\]

At t=1/2, the normalized distribution polynomial P=p_t/8 satisfies

\[
P_{111}=\frac14,\qquad N(P)=\frac{19}{64},
\]

and

\[
c_3N(P)-P_{111}^2=\frac1{288}>0.
\]

This is a counterexample to extending the desired inequality to balanced inputs specified only by marginal ULC or by all binary coefficient log-concavity inequalities. It is not a Lorentzian counterexample and not a Gyires counterexample.

## 4. What the obstruction does and does not establish

The base polynomial p_0 itself satisfies the desired Fock bound strictly:

\[
\frac{([xyz]p_0)^2}{c_3N(p_0)}=\frac98.
\]

It is not a global maximizer after fixing the central coefficient. For example, the one-sided path

\[
p_0+\frac{s}{3}(x^3+y^3+z^3),\qquad0\le s\le1,
\]

is balanced and Lorentzian, keeps the central coefficient 2, and has norm 16+2s². At s=1 it is the uniform power (x+y+z)^3/3, with norm18.

Thus the example does not disprove the possibility that global norm maximizers are uniform powers or belong to a manageable class. It disproves the direct scalar-style claim that every non-binary input can be eliminated by a two-sided supported perturbation. A valid multivariate localization proof must also handle other support faces and the quadratic range-kernel coupling constraints, or provide norm comparisons that eliminate their rigid points.

A precise remaining sufficient target is: characterize the balanced, central-normalized Lorentzian points admitting no nonzero coefficient direction that preserves both signs of every quadratic Hessian condition, and prove the Fock bound on that larger class. That class already contains (4), so binary lifts alone are insufficient for such a characterization.

`round4_localization_obstruction.py` uses only integer and rational polynomial arithmetic. It verifies all three determinant identities, every 2×2 principal-minor polynomial, balance and marginal identities, the norm polynomial, and the exact 1/288 failure at t=1/2. Its output is in `round4_localization_obstruction.json`.
