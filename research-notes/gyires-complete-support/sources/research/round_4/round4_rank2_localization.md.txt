# Round 4: an exact rank-two reduction to a two-interval scalar inequality

## Status and scope

This note proves a representation and a localization theorem. It does **not** prove Gyires's conjecture for all rank-two doubly stochastic matrices, and it does not prove the unrestricted conjecture.

The stronger inequality under study is

\[
 \operatorname{per}(A)^2\ge c_n\operatorname{per}(AA^T),
 \qquad c_n=\frac{n!}{n^n}.\tag{1}
\]

For rank-two doubly stochastic matrices, (1) is exactly a two-law inequality for Poisson-binomial distributions. A sufficient extension of that inequality to ultra-log-concave distributions can be reduced, rigorously, to pairs of tilted binomial laws supported on intervals. The inequality for those interval pairs remains unproved. All reductions below are exact; the numerical probe at the end is not used as a proof.

## 1. A common-mean factorization of every rank-two matrix

Let \(A\) be a nonnegative doubly stochastic \(n\times n\) matrix of rank two. Its rows lie on an affine line in the probability simplex. Choose the endpoints \(r,s\) of the intersection of that line with the simplex, and write its rows as

\[
 A_{ij}=x_i r_j+(1-x_i)s_j,\qquad 0\le x_i\le1.
\]

Put \(t=\sum_i x_i\). Since the rank is two, \(0<t<n\). The column sums give

\[
 tr_j+(n-t)s_j=1.
\]

Consequently \(y_j=tr_j\) satisfies \(0\le y_j\le1\), \(\sum_jy_j=t\), and

\[
 \boxed{\quad
 A_{ij}=\frac{x_i y_j}{t}
       +\frac{(1-x_i)(1-y_j)}{n-t},
 \qquad x,y\in[0,1]^n,\quad
 \sum_i x_i=\sum_i y_i=t.\quad}\tag{2}
\]

Conversely, (2) always defines a nonnegative doubly stochastic matrix of rank at most two. The rank-one case is the uniform matrix \(J_n\), for which (1) is equality. The parameter \(t\) in a rank-two representation need not be an integer.

Write \(\theta=t/n\), and let \(P_x\) be the law of a sum of independent Bernoulli variables with success probabilities \(x_1,\ldots,x_n\). Define \(P_y\) similarly, and let

\[
 B(k)=\binom nk\theta^k(1-\theta)^{n-k},\qquad 0\le k\le n.
\]

All these probability laws have mean \(t\). Since \(0<\theta<1\), \(B(k)>0\). Define likelihood ratios

\[
 r(k)=\frac{P_x(k)}{B(k)},\qquad
 s(k)=\frac{P_y(k)}{B(k)},\qquad
 \langle f,g\rangle_B=\sum_{k=0}^nB(k)f(k)g(k).
\]

## 2. The permanent is an inner product of two likelihood ratios

Expand each matrix entry in (2) into its two summands. If exactly \(k\) rows use the first summand, summing over the column permutations contributes \(k!(n-k)!\) times the corresponding sum over \(k\)-element column sets. Therefore

\[
 \operatorname{per}(A)=
 \sum_{k=0}^n
 \frac{k!(n-k)!}{t^k(n-t)^{n-k}}P_x(k)P_y(k).
\]

Since

\[
 \frac{c_n}{B(k)}
 =\frac{k!(n-k)!}{t^k(n-t)^{n-k}},
\]

we obtain the exact identity

\[
 \boxed{\quad
 \frac{\operatorname{per}(A)}{c_n}=\langle r,s\rangle_B.
 \quad}\tag{3}
\]

This is a two-law identity, rather than the squared norm from the one-special-column case.

## 3. The Gram permanent is a binomial-noise quadratic form

Consider the following reversible Markov chain on \(n\) Bernoulli bits with stationary law \(\operatorname{Bernoulli}(\theta)^{\otimes n}\). Independently for each bit, retain its current value with probability \(\kappa\), and otherwise resample a fresh Bernoulli\((\theta)\) bit. The induced chain on the number of successes is reversible with stationary distribution \(B\). Denote its operator on functions of the count by \(T_\kappa\), for \(0\le\kappa\le1\).

If the initial independent bit parameters are \(x_i\), the new independent parameters are

\[
 x'_i=\theta+\kappa(x_i-\theta).
\]

Reversibility shows that the likelihood ratio of the new count law is \(T_\kappa r\). The count transition only depends on the number of initial successes, so this conclusion applies even when the original bits have unequal parameters.

Define

\[
\begin{aligned}
 \kappa(s)
 &=\frac{n\sum_i y_i^2-t^2}{t(n-t)}\\
 &=1-\frac{\operatorname{Var}(P_y)}
                {t(1-t/n)}.\tag{4}
\end{aligned}
\]

The second equality follows from
\(\operatorname{Var}(P_y)=\sum_i y_i(1-y_i)=t-\sum_i y_i^2\).
It also gives \(0\le\kappa(s)\le1\), because a Poisson-binomial law with mean \(t\) has variance at most \(t(1-t/n)\), by Cauchy–Schwarz applied to the parameters.

Formula (2) can be rewritten as

\[
 A=J_n+\frac{n}{t(n-t)}
       (x-\theta\mathbf1)(y-\theta\mathbf1)^T.
\]

Multiplication gives

\[
 AA^T
 =J_n+\frac{n}{t(n-t)}\kappa(s)
       (x-\theta\mathbf1)(x-\theta\mathbf1)^T.
\]

This is the matrix in (2) formed from \(x\) and
\(x'=\theta\mathbf1+\kappa(s)(x-\theta\mathbf1)\).
Applying (3) proves

\[
 \boxed{\quad
 \frac{\operatorname{per}(AA^T)}{c_n}
      =\langle r,T_{\kappa(s)}r\rangle_B.
 \quad}\tag{5}
\]

Combining (3) and (5), the strong rank-two inequality (1) is equivalent to

\[
 \boxed{\quad
 \langle r,s\rangle_B^2
       \ge\langle r,T_{\kappa(s)}r\rangle_B
 \quad}\tag{R}
\]

for Poisson-binomial laws \(P_x,P_y\) of the same mean \(t\).

If (R) were established for every such pair, applying it also with the pair reversed would establish both Gram bounds. The permanent Cauchy–Schwarz inequality
\(\operatorname{per}(A^2)\le
\sqrt{\operatorname{per}(AA^T)\operatorname{per}(A^TA)}\)
would then imply original Gyires for every rank-two doubly stochastic matrix. The missing assertion is (R), not one of these identities.

## 4. A sufficient scalar extension

Call a probability law \(P\) on \(\{0,\ldots,n\}\) ultra-log-concave of order \(n\) if its support is an interval and

\[
 \frac{P(k)}{\binom nk}
\]

is log-concave on that interval. Equivalently, \(P/B\) is log-concave, because the two normalizations differ by a positive geometric sequence. Newton's inequalities applied to
\(\prod_i((1-x_i)+x_i z)\) show that Poisson-binomial laws have this property.

Consider the stronger scalar assertion that (R) holds for **all** order-\(n\) ultra-log-concave laws \(P,Q\) with mean \(t\), where

\[
 r=P/B,\qquad s=Q/B,\qquad
 \kappa(s)=1-\frac{\operatorname{Var}(Q)}{t(1-t/n)}.\tag{6}
\]

This stronger assertion is unproved. The following sections reduce it exactly to interval-supported geometric likelihood ratios.

### Variance bound for this larger class

For any such \(P\), the likelihood ratio \(r=P/B\) is log-concave. Hence the set of indices on which \(r>1\) is an interval. The signed sequence \(P-B\) has the sign pattern negative, positive, negative, allowing empty or zero portions.

Both its mass and first moment are zero. To compare expectations of a convex function \(\varphi\), take the secant line through the first and last indices where \(P-B>0\). On the interval between them, \(\varphi\) lies below this line; outside it, \(\varphi\) lies above its extension. Multiplication by \(P-B\) makes each difference nonpositive. The line contributes zero because the mass and first moments agree. If there is only one positive index, use a supporting line at that index. Thus

\[
 E_P\varphi(K)\le E_B\varphi(K).
\]

Taking \(\varphi(k)=(k-t)^2\) proves

\[
 0\le \operatorname{Var}(P)\le t(1-t/n).
\]

Therefore (6) always lies in \([0,1]\); the binomial-noise operator in the proposed extension is well defined.

## 5. The deficit is separately convex

Fix \(n,t\). Define

\[
 D(r,s)=
 \sqrt{\langle r,T_{\kappa(s)}r\rangle_B}
       -\langle r,s\rangle_B.\tag{7}
\]

Since all functions here are nonnegative, (R) is equivalent to \(D(r,s)\le0\).

For fixed \(s\), \(T_{\kappa(s)}\) is positive semidefinite. The square root in (7) is a seminorm of \(r\), so \(D\) is convex in \(r\).

To prove convexity in the other argument, use an orthonormal basis \(K_0,\ldots,K_n\) of the binomial count space obtained by symmetrizing the centered Bernoulli product basis. Explicitly, if
\(\xi_i=(X_i-\theta)/\sqrt{\theta(1-\theta)}\), the normalized sum
\[
 \binom nk^{-1/2}
 \sum_{|S|=k}\prod_{i\in S}\xi_i
\]
depends only on the number of successes and defines \(K_k\). These are the normalized Krawtchouk polynomials. Independence gives orthonormality, and the bit-noise construction gives

\[
 T_\kappa K_k=\kappa^kK_k.
\]

Mass one and mean \(t\) imply

\[
 r=1+\sum_{k=2}^n a_kK_k;
\]

the coefficient of \(K_1=(K-t)/\sqrt{t(1-t/n)}\) is zero. Consequently

\[
\begin{aligned}
 \sqrt{\langle r,T_\kappa r\rangle_B}
 &=\sqrt{1+\sum_{k=2}^n a_k^2\kappa^k}\\
 &=\left\|(1,|a_2|\kappa,|a_3|\kappa^{3/2},
                    \ldots,|a_n|\kappa^{n/2})\right\|_2.\tag{8}
\end{aligned}
\]

Every coordinate on the last line is nonnegative and convex for \(\kappa\ge0\). The Euclidean norm is convex and increasing in each coordinate on the nonnegative orthant. Thus (8) is a convex function of \(\kappa\).

On the set of probability laws of fixed mean \(t\), \(\kappa(s)\) in (6) is affine in \(s\). It follows that \(D(r,s)\) is convex in \(s\) for fixed \(r\). This proves separate convexity. No joint convexity is asserted or needed.

## 6. A localization lemma with two linear constraints

Let \(\mathcal U_{n,t}\) be the set of probability laws \(P\) of mean \(t\) for which \(P/B\) is log-concave with interval support. This is a compact set. For example, closedness follows by expressing log-concavity through all of its finite, exponentiated chord inequalities, which also exclude internal zeros between two positive coefficients.

The following elementary lemma applies to any continuous convex functional of \(P\).

### Lemma

A continuous convex functional on \(\mathcal U_{n,t}\) has a maximizer whose likelihood ratio \(u=P/B\) is geometric on its support interval. A singleton support is also permitted when \(t\) is an integer.

### Proof

Choose a maximizer that, among all maximizers, maximizes a strictly convex auxiliary function such as \(\sum_kP(k)^2\). Write its positive support as \([a,b]\).

At every interior index where log-concavity is an equality,

\[
 u_k^2=u_{k-1}u_{k+1},
\]

impose the linear equation

\[
 2h_k=h_{k-1}+h_{k+1}.\tag{9}
\]

If at least one interior log-concavity inequality is strict, the vector space of solutions \(h\) has dimension at least three: the second-difference equations for all interior indices are independent, and omitting one leaves at least one additional degree of freedom beyond the two-dimensional space of affine sequences.

Impose also

\[
 \sum_kP(k)h_k=0,\qquad
 \sum_k kP(k)h_k=0.\tag{10}
\]

There is a nonzero solution \(h\). For sufficiently small positive \(\varepsilon\), define

\[
 P^\pm(k)=P(k)(1\pm\varepsilon h_k).
\]

They preserve positivity on the same support, total mass, and mean. At an equality index, the new log-concavity difference is

\[
\begin{aligned}
 &(u_k(1\pm\varepsilon h_k))^2
   -u_{k-1}u_{k+1}
       (1\pm\varepsilon h_{k-1})(1\pm\varepsilon h_{k+1})\\
 &\hspace{1cm}
 =u_k^2\varepsilon^2
        (h_k^2-h_{k-1}h_{k+1})\\
 &\hspace{1cm}
 =u_k^2\varepsilon^2(h_k-h_{k-1})^2\ge0,
\end{aligned}
\]

where (9) was used. The initially strict inequalities remain valid for sufficiently small \(\varepsilon\). Hence \(P^\pm\in\mathcal U_{n,t}\), and \(P=(P^++P^-)/2\).

Convexity forces both \(P^+\) and \(P^-\) to be maximizers of the original functional. Strict convexity of the auxiliary function forces one of them to have larger auxiliary value than \(P\), a contradiction.

Therefore every interior log-concavity inequality is an equality. The likelihood ratio is geometric on its support. Supports of length one or two already have the asserted form. This proves the lemma.

### Application to the two-law deficit

The continuous function \(D\) has a maximum on
\(\mathcal U_{n,t}\times\mathcal U_{n,t}\). Hold the second law of a maximizing pair fixed and apply the lemma to the first argument, using Section 5. This retains the global maximum and makes the first law geometric on an interval. Next hold that first law fixed and apply the lemma in the second argument. Both laws now have the claimed form.

Consequently, the extended assertion (R) for all of
\(\mathcal U_{n,t}\times\mathcal U_{n,t}\) is **equivalent** to (R) for pairs

\[
 P_I(k)=\frac{B(k)e^{\lambda_I k}\mathbf1_{\{k\in I\}}}{Z_I},
 \qquad
 Z_I=\sum_{j\in I}B(j)e^{\lambda_I j},\tag{11}
\]

and \(P_L\) of the same form, each tilted to mean \(t\).

For a nontrivial interval \(I=[a,b]\), the mean condition has a unique finite solution \(\lambda_I\) precisely when \(a<t<b\); strict monotonicity follows because the derivative of its mean with respect to \(\lambda_I\) is its positive variance. If \(t\) is an integer, the singleton law at \(t\) is also included; its likelihood ratio can be represented with \(\lambda=0\). All limiting endpoint cases are covered by these singleton laws.

For each fixed \(n,t\), there are only finitely many interval pairs to check. The parameter \(t\in(0,n)\) is still continuous, and the interval-pair inequality itself has not been established.

## 7. The precise remaining scalar inequality

For \(0\le\kappa\le1\), define the joint probability kernel

\[
 M_\kappa(k,\ell)=B(k)T_\kappa(k,\ell).
\]

It is specified exactly by

\[
 \sum_{k,\ell}M_\kappa(k,\ell)z^kw^\ell
  =\left[
  (1-\theta)^2+\kappa\theta(1-\theta)
  +\theta(1-\theta)(1-\kappa)(z+w)
  +(\theta^2+\kappa\theta(1-\theta))zw
  \right]^n.\tag{12}
\]

For two laws \(P_I,P_L\) in (11), let

\[
 \kappa_L=1-\frac{\operatorname{Var}(P_L)}
                         {t(1-t/n)}.
\]

After canceling the common \(Z_I^{-2}\), (R) becomes

\[
 \boxed{\quad
 \left[
    \sum_{k\in I\cap L}B(k)
          e^{(\lambda_I+\lambda_L)k}
 \right]^2
 \ \ge\
 Z_L^2
 \sum_{k,\ell\in I}M_{\kappa_L}(k,\ell)
                  e^{\lambda_I(k+\ell)}.
 \quad}\tag{12a}
\]

Here both tilts are constrained to give mean \(t\). Equation (12a), for all \(n,t,I,L\), is the unproved boundary inequality. Proving it would establish the scalar ultra-log-concave extension, and therefore strong and original Gyires for every rank-two doubly stochastic matrix. A failure for these interval laws would disprove that scalar extension; it would not automatically provide a matrix counterexample, because these extremal ultra-log-concave laws need not be Poisson-binomial.

If either law is the full binomial \(B\), (R) is equality: its likelihood ratio is one, and \(T_0r\) is the constant \(E_Br=1\). These cases are useful normalization checks, not evidence for the remaining nontrivial pairs.

## 8. Reproducible numerical scope

The program round4_rank2_scalar_probe.py constructs the interval laws using their unique one-dimensional tilt and computes the joint kernel directly from (12). It examines all oriented nontrivial interval pairs for \(2\le n\le18\) and half-integer means
\[
 t=\tfrac12,1,\tfrac32,\ldots,n-\tfrac12.
\]

The report round4_rank2_scalar_probe.json contains 323 \((n,t)\) cases and no ratio below \(1-10^{-8}\). Its smallest reported ratio is about \(1-4.7\times10^{-15}\), arising from a law numerically extremely close to the full binomial. This is floating-point exploration of the remaining scalar statement. It is not an exact certificate, does not cover all real \(t\), and does not prove (12a).

The separate program round4_rank2_identity_check.py verifies (3), (4), (5), and the count-noise pushforward exactly over rational numbers on examples of orders three, four, and five. One example has the noninteger mean \(t=5/3\). Its report is round4_rank2_identity_check.json. These checks validate implementation and normalization on the specified examples; the general identities are proved analytically above.

The mathematical progress in this note is the exact factorization, permanent/noise identities, separate convexity, and two-interval localization. The rank-two inequality and the unrestricted conjecture remain unresolved here.
