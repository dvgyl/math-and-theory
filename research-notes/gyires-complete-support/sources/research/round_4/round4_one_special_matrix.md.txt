# Both Gram inequalities for a doubly stochastic matrix with one special column

## Theorem

Let $A$ be a nonnegative doubly stochastic matrix of order $n\ge2$ with $n-1$ identical columns. Then

\[
\operatorname{per}(A)^2\ge c_n\operatorname{per}(AA^T),
\qquad
\operatorname{per}(A)^2\ge c_n\operatorname{per}(A^TA),
\qquad c_n=\frac{n!}{n^n}.
\tag{1}
\]

Consequently the original Gyires inequality holds for this family:

\[
4\operatorname{per}(A)^2
\ge c_n\left(\operatorname{per}(AA^T)+\operatorname{per}(A^TA)
+2\operatorname{per}(A^2)\right).
\tag{2}
\]

Equality occurs exactly at $A=J_n$, where every entry of $J_n$ is $1/n$. The same conclusions hold for matrices with $n-1$ identical rows.

The first Gram inequality follows from the scalar ultra-log-concave theorem proved in round4_scalar_ulc.md. The second has the elementary proof below; it does not assume a conjectured bound for the one-success probability.

## 1. Parametrization and the first Gram inequality

Move the special column to the first position. The permanent and both Gram permanents are unchanged by this column relabeling; the final Cauchy–Schwarz step can be applied to the original matrix as well. Every such matrix has columns

\[
u,\ v,\ldots,v,\qquad
u_i\ge0,\quad \sum_i u_i=1,\quad
v_i=\frac{1-u_i}{m},\quad m=n-1.
\]

Its product polynomial is

\[
p_A(x)=\prod_i\left(u_i x_1+v_i(x_2+\cdots+x_n)\right).
\]

It has the one-special-variable form covered by the scalar theorem. It is normalized, balanced, and real stable. Therefore

\[
\operatorname{per}(A)^2\ge c_n\operatorname{per}(AA^T).
\]

## 2. An exact formula for the other Gram permanent

Put

\[
a=\sum_i u_i^2,\qquad
b=u\cdot v=\frac{1-a}{m},\qquad
c=\|v\|^2=\frac{n-2+a}{m^2}.
\]

Then

\[
A^TA=
\begin{pmatrix}
a&b\mathbf1^T\\
b\mathbf1&cE_m
\end{pmatrix}.
\]

In its permanent, either the first row uses the first column, or it uses one of the other $m$ columns. Counting the two cases gives

\[
\operatorname{per}(A^TA)
=m!\,c^{m-1}(ac+mb^2).
\tag{3}
\]

Let

\[
Q(u)=\sum_i u_i\prod_{j\ne i}(1-u_j)
\]

be the probability of exactly one success among independent Bernoulli variables with parameters $u_1,\ldots,u_n$. The permanent is

\[
\operatorname{per}(A)=\frac{m!}{m^m}Q(u).
\tag{4}
\]

After substituting (3) and (4), the second inequality in (1) is equivalent to

\[
Q(u)^2\ge R_n(a),
\tag{5}
\]

where

\[
R_n(a)=
n^{1-n}(n-2+a)^{n-2}\bigl(n-1-na+na^2\bigr),
\qquad \frac1n\le a\le1.
\tag{6}
\]

At $a_0=1/n$,

\[
R_n(a_0)=g_n^2,\qquad
g_n=\left(\frac{n-1}{n}\right)^{n-1}.
\]

## 3. Two elementary lower bounds for the one-success probability

First, the product inequality

\[
\prod_{j\ne i}(1-u_j)\ge1-\sum_{j\ne i}u_j=u_i
\]

gives

\[
\boxed{Q(u)\ge a.}
\tag{7}
\]

For the second bound, initially suppose every $u_i<1$. Weighted AM–GM, with weights $u_i$, gives

\[
\log\left(\sum_i\frac{u_i}{1-u_i}\right)
\ge-\sum_i u_i\log(1-u_i).
\]

Using $Q=\prod_i(1-u_i)\sum_i u_i/(1-u_i)$,

\[
\log Q\ge\sum_i(1-u_i)\log(1-u_i).
\tag{8}
\]

Let $f(x)=(1-x)\log(1-x)$, continuously extended by $f(1)=0$. Since $f''(x)=1/(1-x)\ge1$, strong convexity at $1/n$ gives

\[
\sum_i f(u_i)\ge nf(1/n)+\frac12\sum_i(u_i-1/n)^2
=\log g_n+\frac12(a-1/n).
\]

Therefore

\[
\boxed{Q(u)^2\ge g_n^2e^{a-1/n}.}
\tag{9}
\]

Both bounds extend to boundary points by continuity.

## 4. The range $1/n\le a\le1/2$

Write

\[
d=n-2+a,\qquad K=n-1-na+na^2.
\]

Differentiating (6),

\[
\frac{d}{da}\log R_n(a)
=\frac{n-2}{d}+\frac{n(2a-1)}K\le1
\]

throughout this range. Integrating from $1/n$ gives

\[
R_n(a)\le g_n^2e^{a-1/n}\le Q(u)^2.
\tag{10}
\]

For $n>2$ and $a>1/n$, the first inequality is strict. For $n=2$, this range consists only of the uniform endpoint.

## 5. The range $1/2\le a\le1$

A direct algebraic simplification gives

\[
\frac d{da}\log\frac{R_n(a)}{a^2}
=
\frac{(a-1)\left(n(n-2)a^2+na+2(n-1)(n-2)\right)}
{a(n-2+a)(n-1-na+na^2)}
\le0.
\tag{11}
\]

Thus it is enough to show $R_n(1/2)\le1/4$. At the endpoint,

\[
R_n(1/2)
=\left(1-\frac3{2n}\right)^{n-2}
\left(\frac34-\frac1n\right).
\]

For completeness, set $x=3/(2n)$ and $k=n-2$. The nonnegative binomial series gives

\[
(1-x)^{-k}\ge1+kx+\frac{k(k+1)}2x^2
\ge1+\frac{2k}{n}=3-\frac4n.
\]

The second inequality follows from

\[
\frac{(k+1)x}{2}=\frac{3(n-1)}{4n}\ge\frac13
\]

for $n\ge2$; the case $k=0$ is equality directly. Hence $R_n(1/2)\le1/4$, with strict inequality for $n>2$.

Combining this with (11) and (7),

\[
R_n(a)\le a^2\le Q(u)^2.
\tag{12}
\]

This proves the second Gram inequality in every order.

## 6. Equality and the original Gyires inequality

The preceding bounds are strict unless $a=1/n$. By $\sum_i u_i=1$, that condition forces $u_i=1/n$ for all $i$, so $A=J_n$.

Permanent Cauchy–Schwarz gives

\[
\operatorname{per}(A^2)
\le\sqrt{\operatorname{per}(AA^T)\operatorname{per}(A^TA)}
\le\frac12\left(\operatorname{per}(AA^T)+\operatorname{per}(A^TA)\right).
\]

One direct justification is to expand the row product polynomials of $A$ and $A^T$ as $p_A(x)=\sum_\alpha a_\alpha x^\alpha$ and $p_{A^T}(x)=\sum_\alpha b_\alpha x^\alpha$. The permanent version of Cauchy–Binet gives

\[
\operatorname{per}(A^2)=\sum_\alpha\alpha!a_\alpha b_\alpha,
\quad
\operatorname{per}(AA^T)=\sum_\alpha\alpha!a_\alpha^2,
\quad
\operatorname{per}(A^TA)=\sum_\alpha\alpha!b_\alpha^2.
\]

The displayed bound is therefore ordinary weighted Cauchy–Schwarz.

Applying both inequalities in (1) now proves (2), with equality only at $J_n$. Transposition proves the corresponding statement when $n-1$ rows are identical.

The result is an all-order family theorem. No conclusion about arbitrary rank-two matrices, arbitrary circulants, or arbitrary doubly stochastic matrices is drawn from it.
