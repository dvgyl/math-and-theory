# Round 4: an all-order boundary reduction and the cubic frontier

## Status

The capacity-balanced deletion lemma has not been proved in arbitrary degree, and this note does not claim a proof of Gyires's conjecture. It establishes an all-order reduction for the stronger Lorentzian polynomial norm inequality, and a stability-preserving refinement in degree three. It also derives a concrete convex relaxation for stable ternary cubics. The remaining positivity assertion for that relaxation is unproved.

Write

\[
 c_n=\frac{n!}{n^n},\qquad
 N(p)=\sum_{|\alpha|=n}\alpha!p_\alpha^2,\qquad
 b(p)=[x_1\cdots x_n]p.
\]

For the homogeneous inequalities below, “balanced” means
\(\partial_i p(\mathbf1)=p(\mathbf1)\) for every \(i\). This definition permits an arbitrary positive scalar multiple. Division by \(p(\mathbf1)\) gives the normalized convention.

The proposed stronger polynomial inequality is

\[
 b(p)^2\ge c_nN(p).\tag{P}
\]

For \(p(x)=\prod_i(Ax)_i\) with \(A\) doubly stochastic, it becomes
\(\operatorname{per}(A)^2\ge c_n\operatorname{per}(AA^T)\).

## 1. A proved all-order reduction to a zero pure coefficient

### Theorem

Let \(p\) be a balanced Lorentzian homogeneous polynomial of degree \(n\) in \(n\) variables, with nonnegative coefficients. Put

\[
 S=x_1+\cdots+x_n,\qquad m=\min_i p_{ne_i}.
\]

Then

\[
 q=p-mS^n
\]

has nonnegative coefficients, is balanced and Lorentzian, and has at least one zero pure coefficient. It may be identically zero.

Consequently, (P) holds for every balanced Lorentzian polynomial of degree \(n\) if and only if it holds for all such polynomials with at least one zero pure coefficient.

### Proof of the coefficient assertion

Normalize the coefficients by

\[
 d_\alpha=\frac{p_\alpha}{\binom n\alpha}
             =\frac{\alpha!}{n!}p_\alpha.
\]

The two-by-two principal-minor inequalities for the derivative Hessians give

\[
 d_\alpha^2\ge
 d_{\alpha-e_i+e_j}\,d_{\alpha+e_i-e_j}
 \quad\text{whenever }\alpha_i,\alpha_j>0.\tag{1}
\]

First suppose all coefficients are positive. Among the indices minimizing \(d_\alpha\), choose one maximizing \(\sum_i\alpha_i^2\). If that index has two positive coordinates, (1) and minimality force both neighboring coefficients in (1) to attain the same minimum. At least one neighbor has a strictly larger value of \(\sum_i\alpha_i^2\), a contradiction. Thus the minimum occurs at a pure vertex \(ne_i\). In particular,

\[
 p_\alpha\ge m\binom n\alpha\quad\text{for every }\alpha.\tag{2}
\]

For a general nonnegative Lorentzian polynomial, apply the positive linear substitution

\[
 p_\varepsilon(x)=p(((1-\varepsilon)I+\varepsilon J_n)x),
 \qquad 0<\varepsilon<1.
\]

It preserves Lorentzianity and balance, and makes all degree-\(n\) coefficients positive unless \(p=0\). Passing to the limit in (2) proves it without the positivity assumption. Therefore \(q\) has nonnegative coefficients.

### Proof of Lorentzianity

If \(m=0\), there is nothing to prove. Otherwise, (2) shows that for \(0\le t<m\), every coefficient of

\[
 p_t=p-tS^n
\]

is strictly positive. For every multi-index \(\beta\) of order \(n-2\),

\[
 \operatorname{Hess}(\partial^\beta p_t)
 =\operatorname{Hess}(\partial^\beta p)-t n!\,\mathbf1\mathbf1^T.\tag{3}
\]

The last term is positive semidefinite. Subtracting it cannot increase the number of positive eigenvalues. Thus each matrix in (3) has at most one positive eigenvalue. The full-support characterization of Lorentzian polynomials now implies that \(p_t\) is Lorentzian. Coefficientwise closure, as \(t\uparrow m\), proves the assertion for \(q\).

Balance is linear in the coefficients. Both \(p\) and \(S^n\) are balanced, so \(q\) is balanced. A pure coefficient realizing \(m\) becomes zero.

The derivative-Hessian characterization, coefficientwise closure, and preservation under nonnegative linear substitutions used here are standard results in [Brändén and Huh, *Lorentzian polynomials*](https://annals.math.princeton.edu/wp-content/uploads/annals-v192-n3-p04-s.pdf), especially the full-support criterion and Theorem 2.10. The subtraction argument and its application to (P) are derived here.

### Proof that the boundary subclass suffices

The set

\[
 \mathcal K_n=\{p:b(p)\ge\sqrt{c_n}\,\sqrt{N(p)}\}
\]

is a convex cone, since \(b\) is linear and \(\sqrt{N}\) is a norm. Also,

\[
 b(S^n)=n!,\qquad N(S^n)=n!n^n,
\]

so \(S^n\) lies on its boundary. If the boundary polynomial \(q\) satisfies (P), the triangle inequality gives

\[
\begin{aligned}
 \sqrt{c_nN(p)}
 &\le\sqrt{c_nN(q)}+m\sqrt{c_nN(S^n)}\\
 &\le b(q)+mn!=b(p).
\end{aligned}
\]

The converse is immediate because the boundary subclass is included in the full class.

This is a reduction for the broader Lorentzian statement (P). The polynomial left after subtraction need not remain a product of linear forms, so this is not a reduction solely inside the original matrix-product class. In degrees above three, preservation of real stability has not been asserted.

## 2. In degree three, the subtraction also preserves real stability

Suppose \(p\) is a balanced real-stable ternary cubic with nonnegative coefficients. Fix a real vector \(y\) with \(\sum_i y_i=0\). Homogeneity and balance imply

\[
 p(s\mathbf1+y)=a s^3+u(y)s+v(y),\qquad a=p(\mathbf1)>0.\tag{4}
\]

The coefficient of \(s^2\) is \(\nabla p(\mathbf1)\cdot y=0\). Real stability makes the cubic in (4) real-rooted for every real \(y\). Therefore

\[
 u(y)\le0,\qquad -4u(y)^3\ge27a v(y)^2.\tag{5}
\]

Subtracting \(tS^3\) replaces \(a\) by \(a_t=a-27t\), and leaves \(u,v\) unchanged. Whenever \(a_t>0\), (5) remains valid with \(a_t\) in place of \(a\). Thus the subtracted polynomial is still hyperbolic in direction \(\mathbf1\). Its coefficients remain nonnegative for \(0\le t\le m\), by Section 1. A homogeneous hyperbolic polynomial with nonnegative coefficients is real stable when its hyperbolicity direction lies in the positive orthant. Hence \(p-tS^3\) remains real stable throughout this interval, with the final endpoint handled by closure. If \(a_t=0\) occurs at the endpoint, (2) forces the entire remaining polynomial to be zero.

The hyperbolicity/stability equivalence for nonnegative homogeneous polynomials is recorded in the primary Brändén–Huh paper cited above. The depressed-cubic argument is elementary and specific to degree three.

Consequently, the stable ternary version of (P) is equivalent to its restriction to stable ternary cubics with a zero pure coefficient.

## 3. The same boundary reduction applies to the cubic deletion lemma

Write \(U_i=p_{3e_i}\), \(b=p_{111}\), and

\[
 [x_j]p=a_jx_k^2+b x_kx_\ell+c_jx_\ell^2.
\]

Capacity balancing of a binary quadratic gives
\(H_j=b^2+4a_jc_j\). The average-deletion inequality is exactly

\[
\begin{aligned}
 G(p)&=3\sum_jH_j-4N(p)\\
 &=5b^2-\sum_j\bigl((a_j+c_j)^2+7(a_j-c_j)^2\bigr)
          -24\sum_i U_i^2\ge0.\tag{6}
\end{aligned}
\]

The region defined by (6) and \(b\ge0\) is a convex Lorentz cone in coefficient space. The polynomial \(S^3\) lies on its boundary. Thus Section 1 reduces the all-Lorentzian cubic deletion statement to the subclass with a zero pure coefficient. Section 2 gives the same reduction inside the stable cubic class.

No inequality for that entire boundary subclass has been proved here. The numerically suggested sharp Lorentzian boundary bound \(G\ge(2/3)b^2\) is also unproved. An exact polynomial attaining that proposed constant is given next.

## 4. An exact obstruction to a tempting simplification

One might try to prove separately

\[
 \sum_j\bigl((a_j+c_j)^2+7(a_j-c_j)^2\bigr)\le3b^2.\tag{7}
\]

Indeed, concavity of \(p^{1/3}\) at the balancing point gives
\(U_i\le p(\mathbf1)/27\), while the Lorentzian central-coefficient lower bound gives \(b\ge2p(\mathbf1)/9\). Together they yield \(24\sum U_i^2\le2b^2\), so (7) would imply (6).

However, (7) is false for balanced Lorentzian cubics. The exact example is

\[
 p(x,y,z)=(y+z)\left[
 \frac{(y+z)^2}{4\sqrt3}
 +\frac{x(y+z)}2+\frac{x^2}{\sqrt3}\right].\tag{8}
\]

It has \(b=1\), \(U_x=0\), and is balanced, with
\(p(\mathbf1)=2+4\sqrt3/3\). Its derivative in \(y\) or \(z\) is the square of a linear form. Its derivative in \(x\) factors as
\((y+z)((y+z)/2+2x/\sqrt3)\). These derivatives are stable quadratics, and its support is the degree-three simplex with the vertex \((3,0,0)\) removed, an M-convex support. Therefore (8) is Lorentzian.

Direct calculation gives

\[
 N(p)=\frac{13}{3},\qquad H_x=H_y=H_z=2,
 \qquad G(p)=\frac23,
\]

while the left side of (7) is \(10/3\). Thus the mixed-coefficient estimate fails although the desired deletion inequality holds strictly.

The polynomial (8) is not real stable. As a polynomial in \(x\) and \(s=y+z\), its quadratic factor has discriminant

\[
 \frac14-4\frac1{4\sqrt3}\frac1{\sqrt3}=-\frac1{12}<0.
\]

This distinction matters: the example refutes a Lorentzian proof of (7), not its stable-only version. We did not prove the stable-only version either.

## 5. A concrete convex relaxation for stable ternary cubics

This section supplies a valid reduction, followed by an explicitly unproved positivity problem. It does not assume that every balanced stable cubic is a convex combination of balanced products.

By the Helton–Vinnikov theorem, a normalized balanced stable ternary cubic admits

\[
 p(x,y,z)=\det(xA+yB+zC),\qquad
 A,B,C\succeq0,\quad A+B+C=I_3,\quad
 \operatorname{tr}A=\operatorname{tr}B=\operatorname{tr}C=1.\tag{9}
\]

The definite representation theorem and equality of the represented hyperbolicity cone with the positive-definite matrix cone are stated in [Hanselka, *Definite Determinantal Representations of Ternary Hyperbolic Forms*, Theorem 1](https://arxiv.org/html/1411.1661v2). Congruence normalizes the matrix at \(\mathbf1\) to \(I_3\); the trace statements in (9) follow by differentiating the determinant. The nonnegative orthant lies in the closure of the hyperbolicity cone, which makes the coefficient matrices positive semidefinite.

For now assume \(I-A\) is positive definite. Congruence by its inverse square root, followed by an orthogonal change of basis, gives

\[
 p=\det(I-A)\det\left(xR+
       \operatorname{diag}(\beta_i y+(1-\beta_i)z)\right),
 \qquad 0\le\beta_i\le1.\tag{10}
\]

Let the eigenvalues of \(R\) be \(r_1,r_2,r_3\), and let \(W\) be the matrix of squared entries of an orthogonal eigenvector matrix. Then \(W\) is doubly stochastic. In dimension three, both the diagonal entries of \(R\) and those of \(\operatorname{adj}R\) are linear functions of the same matrix \(W\):

\[
 R_{ii}=\sum_j W_{ij}r_j,
 \qquad
 (\operatorname{adj}R)_{ii}
 =\sum_j W_{ij}\prod_{k\ne j}r_k.\tag{11}
\]

Expanding the determinant in (10) against its diagonal matrix shows that its entire coefficient vector is affine in \(W\). A Birkhoff decomposition of \(W\) therefore yields a convex combination of products

\[
 f_\pi(x,y,z)=\prod_{i=1}^3
 \left(a_{\pi(i)}x+(1-a_{\pi(i)})\beta_i y
                 +(1-a_{\pi(i)})(1-\beta_i)z\right),\tag{12}
\]

where \(a_j=r_j/(1+r_j)\) are the eigenvalues of \(A\). In particular,
\(a_j\in[0,1]\) and \(\sum_j a_j=1\). The constant determinant factor in (10) is exactly absorbed by the row normalizations in (12).

Every \(f_\pi\) has value one at \(\mathbf1\), and its \(x\)-derivative there is one. Its \(y\)-derivative is

\[
 B_\pi=\sum_i (1-a_{\pi(i)})\beta_i,
\]

which need not equal one. Balance of the mixture imposes the single linear equation that the averaged \(B_\pi\) is one.

Thus a convex-cone proof of (6), or of (P), would follow if the desired cone contained every balanced point in this affine image of the Birkhoff polytope. A hyperplane section of a polytope has vertices either at original vertices or on edges. Edges of the order-three Birkhoff polytope join permutation matrices differing by one cycle. It therefore suffices for this **relaxed** proof strategy to handle

\[
 (1-\theta)f_\pi+\theta f_\sigma,
 \qquad (1-\theta)B_\pi+\theta B_\sigma=1,
\]

where \(\pi^{-1}\sigma\) is a transposition or a three-cycle.

This is not yet a proof: the individual products need not be balanced, and the relaxed two-product mixtures need not all be stable. The exact order-three doubly stochastic matrix theorem cannot be applied separately to their summands. The necessary two-product positivity statement remains unproved. The program `round4_cubic_spectral_relaxation.py` is a numerical falsification probe of this statement, and its passing searches are not used as a proof.

On the boundary from Section 2, one eigenvalue of \(A\) can be taken to be zero, leaving \(a=(t,1-t,0)\). This reduces the number of spectral parameters but does not by itself force factorization of the cubic. If an eigenvalue of \(A\) equals one, the other coefficient matrices preserve its orthogonal complement and the polynomial factors; those endpoints can also be handled by continuity.

## 6. Limits of attempted local algebraic certificates

`round4_cubic_sos_search.py` searches several explicitly specified sufficient certificate forms for the full cubic inequality. These include nonnegative multiples of two-by-two Alexandrov–Fenchel minors, derivative-Hessian determinants, balancing-point reverse-Cauchy defects, and exact balance identities. Stable-only variants also use real-rooted Laguerre inequalities, including

\[
 2(\partial_v p(x))^2-3p(x)\partial_v^2p(x)\ge0
\]

for real \(x\) and nonnegative directions \(v\). These follow from Cauchy–Schwarz applied to the real roots of the univariate restriction of a stable cubic.

The tested low-degree certificate forms were infeasible. That does not disprove the desired inequality or rule out other sums-of-squares certificates. It records the precise scope of the unsuccessful algebraic attempt; it is not evidence against the conjecture.

## 7. Independent review of the zero-pure-coordinate obstruction

The exact certificate in `round4_zero_pure_certificate.py` was independently reviewed. For its base matrix, the deleted cubic has capacity scaling \(D=(t,1,t^2)\), where

\[
 f(t)=19t^4+9t^3-15t-15=0.
\]

The three logarithmic balancing numerators reduce exactly to

\[
 (0,-15t f(t),15t f(t)),
\]

and the squared scaled norm is exactly

\[
 101250t^{-4}+202500t^{-2}+372100+72900t^2+162450t^4.
\]

For the perturbation with only one zero matrix entry, the rational interval calculation encloses the map \(x\mapsto x-RF(x)\) strictly inside the proposed positive box. Brouwer's theorem therefore supplies a balancing point, and convexity of logarithmic capacity identifies it as a global minimizer. The resulting upper bound on the deletion-norm ratio is strictly below one. This refutes the rule of selecting only coordinates with zero pure coefficient, even when exactly one such coordinate exists. It does not refute the unrestricted maximum-over-all-coordinates deletion lemma.

## 8. A rigidity statement for a stable cubic on the boundary

There is one useful distinction between the stable boundary and the larger Lorentzian boundary. Suppose \(p(x,y,z)\) is a balanced nonnegative real-stable ternary cubic, its \(x^3\) coefficient is zero, and

\[
 H_x=\operatorname{Hess}(\partial_xp)
\]

is singular. Then \(p\) factors over the reals.

If the degree in \(x\) is at most one, balance and nonnegative coefficients imply that every monomial has \(x\)-exponent exactly one: the mean of an exponent in \(\{0,1\}\) can equal one only in this case. Hence \(p=xq(y,z)\).

Otherwise its degree in \(x\) is two. The derivative \(\partial_xp\) is a stable quadratic whose Hessian is singular and whose \(x^2\) coefficient is zero. A rank-two real quadratic form with at most one positive eigenvalue factors into real linear forms. Stability of its linear factors, together with nonnegative coefficients, permits them to be chosen with nonnegative coefficients. Exactly one of them has a nonzero \(x\) coefficient. Consequently,

\[
 \partial_xp=L(y,z)(cx+M(y,z)),\qquad c>0,
\]

for nonnegative linear forms \(L,M\). Integration gives

\[
 p=L(y,z)\left(\frac c2x^2+M(y,z)x\right)+r(y,z).
\]

For every fixed real \(y,z\) with \(L(y,z)\ne0\), real stability makes this quadratic in \(x\) real-rooted. Its discriminant is therefore

\[
 \Delta=L\bigl(LM^2-2cr\bigr)\ge0.
\]

If \(r\) did not vanish identically on the real line \(L=0\), crossing that line near a point where \(r\ne0\) would change the sign of \(\Delta\). Thus \(L\) divides \(r\), and \(p=LQ\).

The rank-one quadratic case cannot occur in the degree-two-in-\(x\) branch: a rank-one quadratic with a zero \(x^2\) coefficient is independent of \(x\). This covers the possible degeneracy omitted by the rank-two argument.

Thus an irreducible stable boundary cubic with zero \(x^3\) coefficient must have nonsingular \(H_x\). This is a structural restriction, not a proof of the remaining boundary inequality: the other Hessians and the full real-rootedness discriminant can still impose additional active boundary conditions.

## 9. Why uniform-power subtraction cannot be iterated indiscriminately

Section 1 subtracts a very special balanced polynomial: its derivative Hessians are positive semidefinite rank-one matrices. Replacing that polynomial with an arbitrary balanced stable polynomial on a boundary face is invalid, even when coefficientwise nonnegativity is preserved.

Here is an exact rational example:

\[
\begin{aligned}
 p(x,y,z)&=(2y+4z)(2x+3y+z)(4x+y+z),\\
 h(x,y,z)&=\frac18(y+z)(2x+y+z)^2.
\end{aligned}
\]

Both are balanced stable products with nonnegative coefficients and a zero \(x^3\) coefficient. Their values at \(\mathbf1\) are \(216\) and \(4\), respectively, and each of their first derivatives at \(\mathbf1\) equals the corresponding value. Every degree-three coefficient other than \(x^3\) is strictly positive in \(p-h\). Explicitly, the coefficient vectors of \(p\) and \(h\), in the order
\((x^2y,x^2z,xy^2,xyz,xz^2,y^3,y^2z,yz^2,z^3)\), are

\[
 (16,32,28,68,24,6,20,18,4)
\]

and

\[
 \left(\tfrac12,\tfrac12,\tfrac12,1,\tfrac12,
       \tfrac18,\tfrac38,\tfrac38,\tfrac18\right).
\]

Nevertheless,

\[
 \operatorname{Hess}(\partial_x(p-h))
 =\begin{pmatrix}
 0&31&63\\31&55&67\\63&67&47
 \end{pmatrix}.
\]

Its determinant is \(-1760\), and its trace is \(102\). Hence it has two positive eigenvalues and one negative eigenvalue. In particular, \(p-h\) is not Lorentzian.

The obstruction already occurs for arbitrarily small positive subtraction. Indeed,
\[
 \operatorname{Hess}(\partial_xp)
  \begin{pmatrix}-11\\16\\-8\end{pmatrix}=0,
 \qquad
 (-11,16,-8)\operatorname{Hess}(\partial_xh)
     \begin{pmatrix}-11\\16\\-8\end{pmatrix}=-112.
\]
Subtracting a small positive multiple of \(h\) therefore moves the null eigenvalue into the positive half-line while retaining the pre-existing positive eigenvalue.

This refutes a prospective iterative boundary-peeling rule. It is not a counterexample to a permanent inequality or to the capacity-balanced deletion lemma.

## 10. A separate rank-two reduction

The note round4_rank2_localization.md proves an exact scalar representation for every nonnegative rank-two doubly stochastic matrix. It expresses its normalized permanent as an inner product of two Poisson-binomial likelihood ratios and its normalized Gram permanent as a binomial-noise quadratic form. A sufficient ultra-log-concave extension of the resulting inequality admits a rigorous localization to pairs of geometric likelihood ratios supported on intervals.

The two-interval inequality is explicitly left unproved. Thus this provides a smaller, concrete analytic target for the rank-two case; it does not establish the rank-two or unrestricted conjecture.

## What remains

There is now a proved boundary reduction for the broader norm statement in every order, and the reduction remains inside the stable class in degree three. The direct rule “delete a coordinate whose pure coefficient vanishes” is false. No replacement boundary argument, general capacity-balanced deletion lemma, or unrestricted proof of original Gyires has been obtained.

The concrete unresolved cubic question is (6) for all balanced stable cubics with a zero pure coefficient. The determinant/Birkhoff construction supplies one explicit sufficient relaxation of that question, but its two-product inequality has not been established. The sharp-looking Lorentzian boundary inequality \(G\ge(2/3)b^2\) also remains a conjectural intermediate statement.
