## 1. The theorem obtained

Let P_n be the cyclic permutation matrix of order n. For every n>=1 and every a,b,c>=0, put

\[
C=aP_n^{-1}+bI+cP_n,\qquad c_n=\frac{n!}{n^n}.
\]

Then

\[
\boxed{c_n\operatorname{per}(CC^T)\le\operatorname{per}(C)^2.}
\]

Consequently Gyires's original inequality holds for every doubly stochastic member of this family, in every order. For n>=4 the strong inequality is strict unless a=b=c=0. In order three its nonzero equality case is a=b=c; in order two it is b=a+c; order one is immediate.

The theorem also holds with **any permutation matrix R** in place of P_n, by decomposition into cycles and the direct-sum inequality for the constants c_n. Thus it covers three permutation matrices lying in an arithmetic progression within a cyclic permutation group, in arbitrary dimension. It does not cover arbitrary triples of permutation matrices, arbitrary circulants, or arbitrary doubly stochastic matrices.

There are two available rigorous upper-bound proofs:

1. A short explicit-vector argument with the rational constant K=51/20, proved fully below. It requires finite coefficient checks through n=93.
2. The parent's sharper exact Bernstein certificate proves K=5/2 and only requires finite checks through n=69. Its mathematical criterion and implementation were reviewed independently in this subtask; a proof of the criterion is included below.

The finite transfer calculation has passed **all six sorting orders for every n=5,...,100**, using exact Python integers. Separate direct permanent calculations cover n=3,4 and verify the transfer representation in n=1,...,7.

## 2. Permanent of the tridiagonal circulant

Let

\[
\lambda=\frac{b+\sqrt{b^2+4ac}}2,\qquad
\nu=\frac{b-\sqrt{b^2+4ac}}2.
\]

The standard cycle-cover expansion, or the four-state transfer construction below, gives

\[
\operatorname{per}(C)=a^n+c^n+\lambda^n+\nu^n.
\]

This formula includes small n when coincident cyclic diagonals are added in the actual matrix. Define

\[
L=\max(a,c,\lambda).
\]

Then

\[
\operatorname{per}(C)\ge L^n.
\]

The terms a^n and c^n correspond directly to the two cyclic permutations. For the lambda term, if n is even, nu^n>=0. If n is odd, use `|nu|<=sqrt(ac)` and

\[
a^n+c^n\ge2(ac)^{n/2}\ge|\nu|^n.
\]

Thus the possibly negative nu term cannot cancel lambda^n.

If L=0 the theorem is trivial. Otherwise divide a,b,c by L. After this homogeneous normalization,

\[
0\le a,c\le1,\qquad 0\le b\le1-ac.
\]

The second condition is equivalent to lambda<=1, since lambda is the nonnegative root of `x^2-bx-ac`.

## 3. Exact finite-state transfer representation

For a cyclic band matrix with offsets -r,...,r and offset weights w_0,...,w_(2r), use states `m=0,...,2^(2r)-1`. At a row, m records occupied columns in the window ending just before the furthest available column. Choose an unused bit j in `{0,...,2r}`, require the outgoing leftmost column to be occupied, and shift the window:

\[
m\longrightarrow (m\mathbin{\mathrm{OR}}2^j)\mathbin{\mathrm{div}}2
\]

with weight w_j, provided bit j was zero and the new mask has bit zero set before shifting.

The permanent is `tr(T^n)`. One can see the bijection by lifting a cyclic perfect matching to a periodic matching of integer-indexed rows and columns. The occupancy state is periodic; conversely a closed sequence of states assigns each row and column exactly once. When offsets coincide modulo small n, choosing the different lifts contributes precisely the multiplicity in the corresponding matrix entry.

The four-state transfer matrix for C splits into scalar blocks a,c and the block

\[
\begin{pmatrix}b&c\\a&0\end{pmatrix},
\]

which proves the permanent formula above.

For the Gram matrix, set

\[
d=a^2+b^2+c^2,\qquad e=b(a+c),\qquad f=ac.
\]

Its offsets -2,-1,0,1,2 have weights `(f,e,d,e,f)`. The sixteen-state transfer matrix splits by mask population count. The two scalar blocks are f. In the increasing-mask orders `(1,2,4,8)` and `(7,11,13,14)`, the population-one and population-three blocks are transposes of one another. Their representative is

\[
T_1=\begin{pmatrix}
e&d&e&f\\
f&0&0&0\\
0&f&0&0\\
0&0&f&0
\end{pmatrix}.
\]

The middle block, in the mask order `(3,5,6,9,10,12)`, is

\[
T_2=\begin{pmatrix}
d&e&0&f&0&0\\
e&0&e&0&f&0\\
f&0&0&0&0&0\\
0&e&d&0&0&f\\
0&f&0&0&0&0\\
0&0&f&0&0&0
\end{pmatrix}.
\]

Therefore

\[
\operatorname{per}(CC^T)
=\operatorname{tr}(T_2^n)+2\operatorname{tr}(T_1^n)+2f^n.
\]

All transfer entries are nonnegative and nondecreasing in b. In the normalized region it is therefore enough to replace b by `1-ac`.

## 4. A fully analytic bound with K=51/20

Let

\[
K=\frac{51}{20},\qquad k=K-2=\frac{11}{20}.
\]

For b=1-ac, write f=ac and s=a+c. Since `(1-a)(1-c)>=0`,

\[
s\le1+f,\quad
e=(1-f)s\le1-f^2,\quad
d=s^2-2f+(1-f)^2\le2-2f+2f^2.
\]

In particular d<=2, e<=1 and f<=1.

### Central block

Use the positive rational vector

\[
v=(1,k,K^{-1},k,kK^{-1},K^{-2})^T.
\]

We claim `T_2 v<=K v`, entrywise.

The first row satisfies

\[
d+k(e+f)
\le K-(2-k)f(1-f)\le K.
\]

The second row satisfies

\[
(1+K^{-1})e+(k/K)f
\le\frac{K+1}{K}+\frac{k^2}{4K(K+1)}
\le Kk.
\]

The final inequality has exact positive margin

\[
Kk-\left(\frac{K+1}{K}+\frac{k^2}{4K(K+1)}\right)
=\frac{2881}{1448400}>0.
\]

For the fourth row, use the convex quadratic

\[
ke+d/K+f/K^2
\le k(1-f^2)+(2-2f+2f^2)/K+f/K^2.
\]

Its quadratic coefficient is `2/K-k=239/1020>0`, so its maximum on `[0,1]` is at an endpoint. The two endpoint values are `k+2/K` and `2/K+1/K^2`, both below Kk. The exact margins are respectively

\[
\frac{1391}{20400}>0,\qquad
\frac{483161}{1040400}>0.
\]

Rows three, five and six follow immediately from f<=1.

### Side blocks

Use `u=(1,K^-1,K^-2,K^-3)^T`. The first row is at most

\[
1+2/K+1/K^2+1/K^3<K,
\]

with margin `1463381/2653020`. The other rows again use f<=1. Thus `T_1u<=Ku`. The scalar blocks satisfy f<=1<K.

### Consequence for traces

For a nonnegative matrix T and a positive vector v with Tv<=Kv,

\[
(T^n)_{ii}v_i\le(T^nv)_i\le K^nv_i,
\]

so each diagonal entry is at most K^n. Applying this to the blocks above yields, after restoring scale L,

\[
\boxed{\operatorname{per}(CC^T)\le16K^nL^{2n}.}
\]

This argument needs no unproved spectral assertion, no floating-point optimization, and no matrix positivity certificate.

## 5. Reduction to finitely many n

The preceding bounds prove the strong inequality whenever

\[
16K^n\le\frac{n^n}{n!}.
\]

For K=51/20 this holds strictly for every n>=94. The base comparison is the exact integer check

\[
94^{94}20^{94}>16\cdot94!\cdot51^{94}.
\]

Writing `r_n=n^n/(n!K^n)`,

\[
\frac{r_{n+1}}{r_n}=\frac{(1+1/n)^n}{K}.
\]

The numerator is strictly increasing in n, and

\[
20\cdot95^{94}>51\cdot94^{94}.
\]

Hence r_n increases for n>=94. This proves the entire infinite tail, rather than checking larger orders individually.

## 6. Finite exact certificate

For n=3,...,93, consider the integer polynomial

\[
D_n(a,b,c)=n^n\operatorname{per}(C)^2-n!\operatorname{per}(CC^T).
\]

For each of the six orders of a,b,c, write the ordered values as `x`, `x+y`, `x+y+z`. The exact polynomial has no negative coefficients in any of these cases. Therefore D_n>=0 for all nonnegative a,b,c.

The script `round2_circ_transfer.py` constructs each permanent through the transfer matrix, using exact integer polynomial arithmetic. It has in fact checked all six orders for **every n=5,...,100**; the results are in `round2_circ_transfer_results.json`, with the log `round2_circ_transfer100_log.txt`. The coefficient transformations and dynamic-programming construction use no floating-point arithmetic.

The separate script `round2_circ_small_check.py` computes permanents directly from the definition by subset dynamic programming. It verifies equality with both transfer polynomials for n=1,...,7 and checks all six gap chambers for n=3,4. The corresponding results are in `round2_circ_small_check.json`.

Strictness for n>=4 is also checked explicitly: the three pure gap powers correspond to binary triples with one, two, or three nonzero unit weights. Integer numeric transfer recurrences verify all seven nonzero binary triples in n=3,...,100, with strictly positive defect in every n>=4 case. All 686 values are saved in `round2_circ_pure_gap_check.json`. Combined with nonnegativity of every coefficient, positive pure gap coefficients force a strictly positive defect for every nonzero triple in these finite orders. The infinite tail is strict by the strict scalar comparison above.

For n=2, set x=b and y=a+c. Then

\[
\operatorname{per}(C)=x^2+y^2,\qquad
\operatorname{per}(CC^T)=(x^2+y^2)^2+4x^2y^2,
\]

and

\[
\operatorname{per}(C)^2-\tfrac12\operatorname{per}(CC^T)
=\tfrac12(x^2-y^2)^2\ge0.
\]

Order one is immediate.

The computational part is a finite proof certificate, not numerical evidence over sampled a,b,c. Its correctness depends on the exact algorithms being correct. The independent checker `round2_independent_finite.mjs` uses characteristic-polynomial/Newton-trace recurrences and direct gap-coordinate generation; it does not use transfer-matrix powers or the primary gap transformation. It verifies 201 reflection-reduced chambers in orders 3 through 69, totaling 693,249 coefficient positions. All coefficients are nonnegative; all are strictly positive for orders 4 through 69. Every minimum in orders 5 through 69 agrees exactly with the primary calculation. No Lean verification is claimed here.

## 7. Sharper alternative K=5/2 from the parent's certificate

The files `round2_spectral_certificate.py` and `.json` compute all leading principal minors of `5I-2T_1` and `5I-2T_2`, as exact bivariate polynomials in a,c with b=1-ac. Each polynomial has strictly positive tensor-product Bernstein coefficients on `[0,1]^2`, with no subdivision required. The minimum coefficients for sizes in ascending order are:

- Side block: `3,37/3,65,325`.
- Central block: `1,1,5,25,125,609`.

The determinant expansion, signs, transfer entries and Bernstein conversion were reviewed in this subtask and are correct.

Why leading minors suffice: M=5I-2T is a Z-matrix. Positive leading minors give positive pivots in Gaussian elimination without row swaps. Each Schur complement remains a Z-matrix because its off-diagonal entries are an existing nonpositive entry minus a nonnegative product divided by a positive pivot. Thus the triangular factors have positive diagonals and nonpositive off-diagonal entries, so their inverses are nonnegative. Consequently M^-1>=0. Taking `x=M^-1 1>0` gives

\[
Tx=(5x-\mathbf1)/2<(5/2)x.
\]

The same diagonal-entry argument then proves `per(CC^T)<=16(5/2)^nL^(2n)`. It suffices to check n<=69 because

\[
70^{70}2^{70}>16\cdot70!\cdot5^{70},\qquad
2\cdot71^{70}>5\cdot70^{70}.
\]

This is a sharper alternative to the entirely explicit-vector proof above; either route, together with its finite bridge, proves the all-n sparse-family theorem.

## 8. Arbitrary permutation matrices and arithmetic-progression supports

For R with cycle lengths m_1,...,m_s, the matrix `aR^-1+bI+cR` is permutation-similar to a direct sum of the corresponding cycle matrices. Multiply their strong inequalities. The constants satisfy

\[
\prod_i\frac{m_i!}{m_i^{m_i}}\ge\frac{n!}{n^n},\qquad n=\sum_i m_i,
\]

because the multinomial theorem gives

\[
1=\left(\sum_i\frac{m_i}{n}\right)^n
\ge\frac{n!}{\prod_i m_i!}\prod_i\left(\frac{m_i}{n}\right)^{m_i}.
\]

This proves the stated extension. For a circulant with three offsets `s-h,s,s+h`, factor out `P_n^s`; permanent and Gram-permanent invariance under row/column permutations reduces to R=P_n^h. Thus h need not be a unit modulo n.

For a nonnegative doubly stochastic matrix in the family, the original Gyires inequality follows by applying the strong inequality to the matrix and its transpose, together with

\[
2\operatorname{per}(A^2)\le\operatorname{per}(AA^T)+\operatorname{per}(A^TA).
\]

## 9. What this does not establish

There is still no proof here for all nonnegative circulants or all doubly stochastic matrices. The all-order sorted-gap coefficient assertion for arbitrary circulant weights remains unproved. Positive finite calculations cannot establish it.

The simpler coefficientwise van der Waerden assertion for nested disjoint permutation layers was tested exactly on all circulant sorting chambers for n=6,7 and selected chambers for n=8,9,10. It passed, but no general proof or implication strong enough for the Gram inequality was found.

A limited structural fact can be proved uniformly. Let B be a Latin-square weight matrix whose every row and column is a permutation of w, and let `F(A)=n^n per(A)^2-n!per(AA^T)`. In the expansion of F(tE+B), the first nonzero powers are

\[
\frac{F(tE+B)}{n^n n!(n-2)!}
=t^{2n-2}\sum_{i<j}(w_i-w_j)^2+t^{2n-3}H_3(w)+\text{lower powers of }t,
\]

where for n>=3,

\[
H_3(w)=\left(\frac{2(n-1)}n-\frac8{3n(n-2)}\right)
\left(\sum_iw_i\right)\sum_{i<j}(w_i-w_j)^2
+\frac4{3(n-2)}\sum_{i<j}(w_i-w_j)^2(w_i+w_j).
\]

Both scalar factors are nonnegative. Therefore these entire quadratic and cubic gap-degree layers are coefficientwise nonnegative after sorting w, independently of the Latin-square arrangement and of n. This explains part of the observed pattern, but leaves all higher gap degrees uncontrolled and cannot supply the full theorem by itself.
