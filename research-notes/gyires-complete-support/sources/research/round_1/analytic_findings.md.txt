# Analytic attempts toward the general Gyires conjecture

Date: 26 October 2026. AI-generated research notes. These notes do **not** establish the unrestricted conjecture. Statements labelled proved below have ordinary mathematical proofs; none of the new material in this note has been formalized in Lean. No novelty or priority claim is made.

Write \(c_n=n!/n^n\) and let \(J_n\) have every entry \(1/n\). The stronger sufficient target is

\[
\operatorname{per}(A)^2\ge c_n\operatorname{per}(AA^T)
\tag{S}
\]

for every doubly stochastic \(A\). Applying it to \(A\) and \(A^T\), together with permanent Cauchy–Schwarz, implies the original Gyires inequality. The current repository already contains this reduction.

## 1. A full tensor reformulation, but no proof from it

Let \(u=n^{-1/2}(1,\ldots,1)\), and let

\[
v=\frac1{\sqrt{n!}}\sum_{\sigma\in S_n}
e_{\sigma(1)}\otimes\cdots\otimes e_{\sigma(n)}.
\]

Then \(\|v\|=1\), \(\langle v,u^{\otimes n}\rangle=\sqrt{c_n}\), and direct expansion gives

\[
\operatorname{per}(A)=\langle v,A^{\otimes n}v\rangle,
\qquad
\operatorname{per}(AA^T)=\|(A^T)^{\otimes n}v\|^2.
\]

Put \(h=(A^T)^{\otimes n}v\). Doubly stochasticity preserves the uniform tensor, so \(\langle h,u^{\otimes n}\rangle=\sqrt{c_n}\). The strong conjecture is exactly the assertion that the angle between \(h\) and \(v\) has cosine at least \(\sqrt{c_n}\).

This is not automatic from nonnegative tensor coordinates, the preserved uniform component, and balanced first moments. The occupancy law that puts mass \(1/n\) on each of \((n,0,\ldots,0),\ldots,(0,\ldots,0,n)\) has mean \((1,\ldots,1)\) but no mass at \((1,\ldots,1)\). It violates the proposed norm bound while satisfying those relaxed conditions. Such a law is not generated by independent categorical row choices. A successful tensor or entropy proof must therefore retain the product structure, or a sufficiently strong consequence such as real stability.

## 2. Two exact obstructions to tempting proof shortcuts

### 2.1 The central coefficient does not dominate every repeated-column coefficient

For

\[
A=\frac14\begin{pmatrix}1&1&2\\0&2&2\\3&1&0\end{pmatrix}
\]

all row and column sums are one, but

\[
\operatorname{per}(A)=\frac5{16},\qquad
\operatorname{per}(A_{[:,(1,3,3)]})=\frac38>\frac5{16}.
\]

If \(P_A(x)=\prod_i\sum_j a_{ij}x_j=\sum_\alpha p_\alpha x^\alpha\), then \(\alpha!p_\alpha\) is the permanent with columns repeated according to \(\alpha\). Consequently the pointwise inequality \(\alpha!p_\alpha\le p_{(1,\ldots,1)}\) is false even in order three. It cannot be used to obtain the desired weighted squared-norm inequality by summing over \(\alpha\).

This matrix is not a counterexample to (S): \(\operatorname{per}(AA^T)=17/64\), and its strong defect is \(89/2304>0\).

### 2.2 Pairwise row averaging need not lower the strong ratio

Let

\[
A=\frac12\begin{pmatrix}1&0&1\\1&1&0\\0&1&1\end{pmatrix}.
\]

Averaging rows one and three produces

\[
B=\frac14\begin{pmatrix}1&1&2\\2&2&0\\1&1&2\end{pmatrix}.
\]

Exact calculations give

\[
\operatorname{per}(A)=\operatorname{per}(B)=\frac14,
\quad \operatorname{per}(AA^T)=\frac14,
\quad \operatorname{per}(BB^T)=\frac{15}{64}.
\]

Thus

\[
\frac{\operatorname{per}(A)^2}{\operatorname{per}(AA^T)}
=\frac14
<\frac4{15}
=\frac{\operatorname{per}(B)^2}{\operatorname{per}(BB^T)}.
\]

This rules out a proof in which arbitrary pairwise row averaging is asserted always to lower the ratio toward its value at \(J_n\). It does not rule out a more carefully chosen sequence of transformations.

## 3. Rank two: an exact lower-dimensional formulation

Every real rank-at-most-two matrix with row and column sums one can be written

\[
A_{ij}=\frac{1+x_i y_j}{n},\qquad
\sum_i x_i=\sum_j y_j=0.
\]

For a doubly stochastic matrix the additional inequalities are \(1+x_i y_j\ge0\). Let \(e_k\) denote elementary symmetric functions and define

\[
F_n(x,y)=\sum_{k=0}^n\frac{e_k(x)e_k(y)}{\binom nk}.
\]

Expanding by the rows and columns that use the rank-one perturbation yields

\[
\operatorname{per}(A)=c_nF_n(x,y).
\]

Since

\[
AA^T=\frac1n\left(\mathbf1\mathbf1^T+
\frac{\|y\|^2}{n}xx^T\right),
\]

the rank-two case of (S) is exactly

\[
F_n(x,y)^2\ge
\sum_{k=0}^n\frac{e_k(x)^2}{\binom nk}
\left(\frac{\|y\|^2}{n}\right)^k.
\tag{R2}
\]

The condition \(1+x_i y_j\ge0\) matters. Centering and contraction alone cannot replace it. For example in order three, take \(x=(\sqrt2,-1/\sqrt2,-1/\sqrt2)\), \(y=-x\). The resulting row- and column-normalized matrix has a negative entry; its nontrivial singular value is one, but \(F_3=5/4\) and the normalized Gram permanent is \(9/4\), so (R2) fails.

### A recent theorem and the still-unproved bridge

Kunisky, Spielman and Yu, *Inequalities for rank-two permanents and finite free convolutions* (28 August 2026), Theorem 1.2, prove

\[
\operatorname{per}(A)^2\ge
\frac{\operatorname{per}(A\otimes\mathbf1_{2\times2})}{\binom{2n}{n}}
\]

for every real matrix of rank at most two, even with negative entries. Their proof gives a sum-of-squares identity. Their Remark 1.3 explicitly gives a nonnegative rank-three counterexample to this sharpened duplication inequality, so the rank assumption cannot be dropped.

Primary source: <https://arxiv.org/html/2608.28520v1>.

This suggests proving the separate bridge

\[
\frac{\operatorname{per}(A\otimes\mathbf1_{2\times2})}{(2n)!}
\ge\frac{\operatorname{per}(AA^T)}{n!n^n}.
\tag{B}
\]

Together these two inequalities would establish (S) for rank two. If \(x^{[2]}\) denotes the vector in which every coordinate of \(x\) occurs twice, then (B) is

\[
F_{2n}(x^{[2]},y^{[2]})\ge
F_n\left(x,\frac{\|y\|^2}{n}x\right).
\]

No proof of this bridge was found. A reproducible diagnostic tested 1,000 random admissible centered pairs at each of orders 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 10, 15, and 20 using elementary-symmetric recurrences in extended floating precision; neither (R2) nor (B) was violated. These are limited numerical tests, not a theorem. The temporary diagnostic is `/tmp/rank2_probe.py`.

## 4. A standard all-order family that does satisfy the strong inequality

If \(A\) is symmetric positive semidefinite and doubly stochastic, all its eigenvalues lie in \([0,1]\), hence \(0\preceq A^2\preceq A\). The permanent is monotone in the positive-semidefinite order: for \(0\preceq X\preceq Y\), tensoring successively shows \(X^{\otimes n}\preceq Y^{\otimes n}\), and evaluating at the tensor \(v\) above gives \(\operatorname{per}(X)\le\operatorname{per}(Y)\). Therefore

\[
c_n\operatorname{per}(A^2)\le c_n\operatorname{per}(A)
\le\operatorname{per}(A)^2,
\]

using the van der Waerden lower bound in the last step. This is an elementary consequence of standard results, not a claimed new theorem. Independent row and column permutations preserve both one-sided strong inequalities, so the conclusion also applies to matrices that can be turned into a symmetric positive-semidefinite doubly stochastic matrix by such permutations.

## 5. Independent review of the three-exceptional-row theorem

I reviewed the full derivation in `combinatorial_findings.md`, separately checking its critical steps:

- The exact coefficient \(M_3(B)=\frac23\sum b_{ij}^3\) follows from the two partition Möbius coefficients, each equal to two, and division by \(3!\).
- For a centered triple whose entries are at least \(-1/n\), its cubic sum is at least minus its squared sum divided by \(2n\). The equality pattern \((-1/n,1/(2n),1/(2n))\) checks the constant.
- The bound \(\|B\|_F^2\le6/n\) follows column by column from the fixed sum of the three exceptional entries.
- For a centered positive-semidefinite three-by-three matrix \(D\), the bound \(\sum d_{ij}^3\le(\operatorname{tr}D)^3/6\) follows from the stated trilinear estimate. I checked the restriction matrix and its eigenvalues \(\pm\|x\|/\sqrt6\).
- The final one-variable lower bound decreases on the required interval; its endpoint constant is positive for all \(n\ge5\), with \(K_5=149/864\).
- The additional restriction to at most three exceptional columns is needed for the provided proof of the original Gyires inequality; the note correctly retains that condition.

No mathematical gap was found in that ordinary proof. This review is independent in-session reasoning by another AI agent, not independent expert review or Lean kernel verification.

### Further review: three exceptional rows without a column restriction, for n >= 31

The subsequent extension removes the exceptional-column assumption when \(n\ge31\). I independently checked its additional estimate as follows.

Let \(D=VV^T\) be any real positive-semidefinite centered matrix of order \(n\), with \(S=\operatorname{tr}D\). Choose \(V\) with \(\sum_i v_i=0\), where \(v_i\) are its rows, and let \(Z\) have independent standard **complex** Gaussian coordinates, normalized by \(\mathbb E|Z_j|^{2k}=k!\). Put \(Y=VZ\). Then \(\sum_iY_i=0\), and, writing \(h_k=\mathbb E|e_k(Y)|^2\), the permanent Gram expansion gives

\[
\frac{\operatorname{per}(J_n+D)}{c_n}
=\sum_{k=0}^n\frac{n^k}{(n)_k}h_k.
\]

For each realization of \(Y\), centering and \(1+t\le e^t\) imply

\[
\left|\prod_i(1+zY_i)\right|\le
\exp\left(\frac{|z|^2\|Y\|^2}{2}\right).
\]

Cauchy's coefficient estimate at radius \(\sqrt{k}/\|Y\|\) therefore yields \(|e_k(Y)|^2\le(e/k)^k\|Y\|^{2k}\). If \(\lambda_j\) are the eigenvalues of \(D\), Gaussian moments give

\[
\mathbb E\|Y\|^{2k}
=k!\sum_{|\eta|=k}\prod_j\lambda_j^{\eta_j}
\le k!S^k.
\]

Using \(\binom nk\ge(n/k)^k\), every normalized degree-\(k\) contribution is at most \((eS)^k\). The degree-one contribution vanishes. Keeping the exact degree-two and degree-three terms improves the bound to

\[
\frac{\operatorname{per}(J_n+D)}{c_n}
\le1+\alpha S^2+\beta S^3+
\frac{(eS)^4}{1-eS},\qquad eS<1,
\]

where \(\alpha=n/[2(n-1)]\) and \(\beta=2n^2/[3(n-1)(n-2)]\). Here \(\sum d_{ij}^2\le S^2\), while

\[
0\le\sum_{ij}d_{ij}^3
=\left\|\sum_i v_i^{\otimes3}\right\|^2
\le\left(\sum_i\|v_i\|^3\right)^2\le S^3.
\]

For a doubly stochastic \(A\) with at most three exceptional rows, the earlier argument gives \(\operatorname{per}(A)/c_n\ge1+aS\) and \(S\le6/n\). Apply the generic Gram bound to both \(BB^T\) and \(B^TB\), where \(B=A-J_n\). Their traces are the same \(S\), regardless of how many columns of \(B\) are nonzero.

For \(n\ge31\), one has \(a\ge1/2\), \(a^2-\alpha\ge-1/3\), and \(\beta\le3/4\). Using \(e<11/4\), the normalized strong defect for either Gram matrix is at least

\[
S\left[1-\frac S3-\frac34S^2-
\frac{(11/4)^4S^3}{1-(11/4)S}\right].
\]

The bracket decreases on \(0\le S\le6/31\), and exact rational evaluation at the endpoint gives

\[
\gamma=\frac{9301}{445904}>0.
\]

Therefore **both** strong inequalities have margin at least \(c_n^2\gamma\|A-J_n\|_F^2\), and original Gyires has margin at least \(4c_n^2\gamma\|A-J_n\|_F^2\), for every such \(A\) in every order \(n\ge31\). Equality occurs only at \(J_n\). I verified the rational endpoint and all estimate directions. This is still an ordinary mathematical proof, not a Lean-verified theorem, and novelty has not been assessed.

## Conclusion of this analytic investigation

The unrestricted strong inequality, the unrestricted original Gyires inequality, and the full rank-two bridge (B) remain unproved in these notes. The exact counterexamples above invalidate two specific proposed general proof shortcuts, while the three-exceptional-row argument survives adversarial review. The rank-two formulation gives a precise smaller problem for future work, but numerical support is not substituted for the missing estimate.
