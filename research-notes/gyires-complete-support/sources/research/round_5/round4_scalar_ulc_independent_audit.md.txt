# Independent audit of the scalar ULC theorem

## Verdict and scope

I independently checked the proof in `round4_scalar_ulc.md`, including the perturbation step, compactness, the truncated-binomial estimate, equality, and the polynomial application. I found no mathematical gap. This is an independent mathematical review by another AI agent, not formal proof-assistant verification or a claim of novelty.

The theorem states that, for B=Bin(n,1/n) and a mean-one probability mass function P on 0,…,n whose likelihood ratio r=P/B is log-concave with interval support,

\[
\sum_k B_kr_k^2\le r_1^2,
\]

with equality only for P=B. Its all-order one-special-variable Lorentzian polynomial application is valid. It does not prove the general multivariate Fock inequality or unrestricted Gyires.

## 1. Normalization and compactness

The interval support and mean-one condition force P_1>0. After setting u=r/r_1, the constraints are u_1=1 and

\[
-B_0u_0+\sum_{k\ge2}(k-1)B_ku_k=0.
\]

If u_0=0, all u_k for k≥2 vanish, so the support is exactly {1}. If u_0>0, the support must be0,…,L for some L≥2.

Since B itself has mean one,

\[
B_0=\sum_{k\ge2}(k-1)B_k.
\]

Thus u_0 is a weighted average of the u_k for k≥2. If u_0>1, log-concavity and u_1=1 give u_k≤u_0^(1−k)<1 for every k≥2 in the support; values outside are zero. This contradicts the weighted-average identity. Therefore u_0≤1, and the mean equation bounds every u_k for k≥2. All coordinates lie in a fixed bounded box.

For closedness, the relevant inequalities include every multi-step log-concavity inequality

\[
u_j^{\,k-i}\ge u_i^{\,k-j}u_k^{\,j-i}
\qquad(0\le i<j<k\le n).
\]

These are continuous polynomial inequalities. They imply that a limiting sequence cannot have an internal zero between two positive endpoint values. Hence interval support is preserved in the limit, and the feasible set is compact. This addresses the main potential boundary concern in the localization step.

## 2. The two-sided perturbation is valid

On support 0,…,L there are L−1 interior second-difference constraints. The equations

\[
2h_k=h_{k-1}+h_{k+1}
\]

at active log-concavity equalities are linearly independent: they are a subset of the independent rows of the path second-difference matrix. If at least one inequality is strict, their solution space has dimension at least three. The two additional homogeneous linear conditions h_1=0 and

\[
\sum_k(k-1)B_ku_kh_k=0
\]

leave a nonzero solution, whether or not the two added conditions are independent.

The perturbations u_k^±=u_k(1±εh_k) preserve both linear feasibility constraints exactly. For sufficiently small ε they remain positive on the same support. At an active equality, dividing the perturbed log-concavity gap by the original positive u_k² gives

\[
(1\pm\varepsilon h_k)^2-
(1\pm\varepsilon h_{k-1})(1\pm\varepsilon h_{k+1})
=\varepsilon^2(h_k-h_{k-1})^2\ge0.
\]

Every formerly strict inequality remains strict for small enough ε. Endpoint inequalities adjoining zeros remain automatic. Thus both perturbations are feasible.

The objective is a positive-definite diagonal quadratic form. Its average at the two perturbations exceeds its value at u by

\[
\varepsilon^2\sum_kB_ku_k^2h_k^2>0.
\]

This is a contradiction to maximality even though the feasible set itself is not convex. No unjustified convexity of the log-concave sequence set is used.

Consequently every nondegenerate maximizer is geometric on its support, u_k=t^(k−1). The mean equation gives its unique positive tilt t. The full support case has t=1; every proper truncation has t>1.

## 3. The uniform tilt estimate

The coefficient bound

\[
\frac{B_k}{B_0}=\frac{\binom nk}{(n-1)^k}
\le\frac{n}{n-1}\frac1{k!}
\]

is correct: after the first falling-factorial factor n/(n−1), the second factor is one and all later ones are at most one. Also

\[
\sum_{k>L}\frac{k-1}{k!}=\frac1{L!}
\]

by telescoping. The derivative of the truncated mean polynomial on t≥1 is at least 2c_2=n/(n−1). These facts give exactly

\[
0\le t_L-1\le1/L!.
\]

This estimate includes the full-support equality case t_n=1.

## 4. The excess-versus-tail comparison

For f(z)=F_L(z)/z, substitution of the balancing equation for B_0 yields

\[
f(t^2)-f(1)=\sum_{k=3}^LB_k
\bigl[t^{2k-2}-1-(k-1)t^{k-2}(t^2-1)\bigr].
\]

I checked this identity directly. The k=2 term cancels identically, so proper cutoff L=2 is settled exactly by f(t²)=F_L(1)<1.

For k≥3, put r=k−1 and s=logt. The bracket equals

\[
2e^{rs}\bigl(\sinh(rs)-r\sinh s\bigr).
\]

Taylor's integral remainder gives sinh(rs)−rs≤(r³s³/6)cosh(rs), while sinh s≥s and e^((r+1)s)−1≥(r+1)s. Therefore the ratio of this bracket to (k−1)(t^k−1) is at most

\[
\frac{r^2}{3(r+1)}e^{2rs}s^2
\le\frac r3e^{2rs}s^2.
\]

The tilt estimate implies s≤1/L!, r≤L−1, and hence 2rs≤2/3 for L≥3. Using e^(2/3)<2 gives

\[
\frac{\psi_k(t)}{(k-1)(t^k-1)}
<\frac{2(L-1)}{3(L!)^2}<\frac1{L+1}.
\]

The last inequality follows from L!≥L, since 2(L−1)(L+1)<3L².

The complementary tail bound is also correct. Set a_i=B_(L+1+i) and q=1/(L+2). The binomial coefficient-ratio estimate implies a_(i+r)≤q^r a_i. Summing over i≥0 gives

\[
\Pr(K-(L+1)\ge r\mid K>L)\le q^r.
\]

Therefore the conditional mean excess is at most q/(1−q)=1/(L+1), and

\[
\frac{T}{M}=\mathbb E[K-1\mid K>L]
\le L+\frac1{L+1}<L+1.
\]

Combining the two bounds proves f(t²)−f(1)<T/(L+1)<M. Since f(1)=1−M, every proper geometric truncation has objective strictly below one. There is no hidden asymptotic approximation in this argument.

## 5. Equality and small-order endpoints

The degenerate support {1} has objective B_1<1 for every n≥2. For n=2, the only nondegenerate geometric candidate has L=n=2 and t=1, so the argument includes this endpoint without needing the L≥3 tail bound.

Any nongeneric equality case at objective one is still subject to the two-sided perturbation unless all its interior log-concavity inequalities are equalities. Thus equality forces full support and t=1. Original probability normalization then forces r_1=1 and P=B.

## 6. The polynomial lift

For

\[
p(x)=\sum_{k=0}^na_kx_1^k(x_2+\cdots+x_n)^{n-k},
\]

normalization gives P_k=a_k(n−1)^(n−k). Balance is equivalent to E K=1. Moreover

\[
r_k=\frac{P_k}{B_k}=\frac{n^na_k}{\binom nk},
\]

so the binary Lorentzian log-concavity condition is exactly the hypothesis of the scalar theorem.

The central coefficient is a_1(n−1)!, so its ratio to c_n=n!/n^n is r_1. For exponent k on the special variable, summing the Fock contributions over all exponents on the other n−1 variables gives

\[
k!((n-k)!)^2a_k^2
\sum_{|\alpha|=n-k}\frac1{\alpha!}
=k!(n-k)!a_k^2(n-1)^{n-k}.
\]

This equals c_n B_k r_k². Thus both identities connecting the scalar inequality to the multivariate Fock inequality are exact.

The one-dimensional theorem cannot simply be applied independently to arbitrary multivariate coordinate conditionals: those conditionals generally have different means, and the full Fock norm retains their dependence. That is the remaining distinction between this verified all-order family theorem and a full solution.
