# A direct boundary reduction for the strong Gram inequality

## Status and definitions

This note proves a reduction in every order. It does not prove the inequality on the remaining boundary. No claim of novelty or formal verification is made.

For a doubly stochastic matrix A of order n, put

\[
c_n=n!/n^n,\qquad p_A(x)=\prod_{i=1}^n(Ax)_i,
\qquad \|p\|_F^2=\sum_{|\alpha|=n}\alpha!p_\alpha^2.
\]

The permanent Gram identity is

\[
\|p_A\|_F^2=\operatorname{per}(AA^T),\qquad
[x_1\cdots x_n]p_A=\operatorname{per}(A).
\]

Define the continuous deficit

\[
F(A)=\sqrt{c_n}\|p_A\|_F-\operatorname{per}(A).
\]

The strong Gram inequality is exactly F(A) <= 0. It implies original Gyires after applying it to A and its transpose and using permanent Cauchy--Schwarz.

## Theorem

For every fixed n >= 2, the strong Gram inequality holds for all doubly stochastic matrices of order n if and only if it holds for all such matrices with at least one zero entry.

If it holds on the boundary, every strictly positive matrix other than J_n satisfies it strictly. In particular, strict validity on the boundary implies that J_n is the only equality case in the whole polytope.

More precisely, any global maximizer of F has the following property: two rows with the same support must be identical.

## Proof

Suppose rows r and s have identical support and are distinct. Write

\[
\mu=(r+s)/2,\qquad d=(r-s)/2.
\]

They both have row sum one, so sum(d)=0. Let T be the largest number such that mu+t*d and mu-t*d are both nonnegative for 0 <= t <= T. Explicitly,

\[
T=\min_{j:d_j\ne0}\frac{\mu_j}{|d_j|}.
\]

Identical supports and r != s give T > 1. Replacing the two rows by mu+t*d and mu-t*d preserves all row and column sums. Denote the resulting matrix by A(t). Its row-product polynomial is

\[
p_{A(t)}(x)=\bigl((\mu\cdot x)^2-t^2(d\cdot x)^2\bigr)
\prod_{\ell\ne r,s}(A x)_\ell.
\]

Consequently it is affine in tau=t^2. For 0 <= tau <= T^2,

\[
p_{A(\sqrt\tau)}
=\left(1-\frac{\tau}{T^2}\right)p_{A(0)}
+\frac{\tau}{T^2}p_{A(T)}.
\tag{1}
\]

Both endpoint polynomials have value one at x=(1,...,1), because the corresponding matrices are row stochastic. They are different: their difference is the nonzero polynomial

\[
-T^2(d\cdot x)^2\prod_{\ell\ne r,s}(A x)_\ell.
\]

Thus they cannot be positive scalar multiples. The Hilbert norm is strictly convex on the segment joining them. Subtracting the linear central-coefficient functional preserves strict convexity. At the original matrix (tau=1), (1) therefore yields

\[
F(A)<\left(1-\frac1{T^2}\right)F(A(0))
+\frac1{T^2}F(A(T)).
\tag{2}
\]

In particular, such a matrix cannot globally maximize F.

The Birkhoff polytope is compact, so F has a global maximum. If a maximizing matrix is strictly positive, all its row supports coincide. The preceding argument forces all its rows to coincide. Column stochasticity then forces this matrix to be J_n, at which F(J_n)=0. Hence

\[
\max_{A\in\Omega_n}F(A)
=\max\left\{0,\max_{A\in\partial\Omega_n}F(A)\right\}.
\tag{3}
\]

This proves the equivalence. If the boundary bound is known, (3) says F <= 0 everywhere; applying the strict inequality (2) to any positive nonuniform A then gives F(A)<0. The equality statement follows as stated.

## What this does and does not change

This argument stays inside the class of doubly stochastic matrices and their products of linear forms. It needs no Lorentzian subtraction or closure assertion.

The boundary still has dimension (n-1)^2-1 on each facet. The theorem gives no induction from n to n-1, and it does not say that it is enough to test permutation matrices or face centers. Rows with different supports need not admit T>1; that is exactly where this argument stops.

The current source repository and Chris Ozols's project already report the strong inequality in the whole order-four polytope. Accordingly, the theorem is not presented as a new proof of an otherwise open order-four case.

## An exact obstruction to a stronger averaging shortcut

Even for rank-two doubly stochastic matrices, averaging two columns can increase the permanent. Take

\[
A=\frac1{30}
\begin{pmatrix}4&15&11\\4&15&11\\22&0&8\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\widetilde A=\frac1{30}
\begin{pmatrix}4&13&13\\4&13&13\\22&4&4\end{pmatrix}.
\]

The second matrix averages columns two and three. Both have rank two, and

\[
\operatorname{per}(A)=137/450,
\quad \operatorname{per}(\widetilde A)=689/2250,
\quad \operatorname{per}(\widetilde A)-\operatorname{per}(A)=2/1125>0.
\]

This refutes Schur-convexity of the permanent in the rank-two column parameter, and rules out proving general rank-two Gram contraction by claiming that every doubly stochastic column mixing decreases the permanent. It does not refute the conjectured comparison with the specific product AA^T.

The same example also prevents a direct multivariate extension of the scalar Poisson-binomial likelihood-ratio peak theorem. Repeating its second column and omitting its third gives

\[
\operatorname{per}(A_{[1,2,2]})=11/30>137/450=\operatorname{per}(A).
\]

Thus the occupancy likelihood ratio can be larger away from the all-ones occupancy even for a rank-two doubly stochastic matrix. The one-dimensional peak theorem remains valid as proved in the scalar note.
