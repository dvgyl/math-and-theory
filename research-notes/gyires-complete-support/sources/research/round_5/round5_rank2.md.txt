# Rank-two analysis, round 5: proved reductions and the remaining inequality

## Status

This note does **not** prove Gyires's conjecture for arbitrary matrices or for all rank-two matrices. It proves several structural facts and isolates two explicit inequalities which would finish the rank-two case. Neither of those inequalities is proved here.

The principal proved statements are:

1. A convex functional of a Poisson-binomial law with prescribed mean has a maximizer among shifted binomial laws. This reduces the rank-two strong inequality to an explicit family with integer multiplicities and one real parameter.
2. If a Poisson-binomial law has integer mean, its likelihood ratio relative to the binomial law of the same mean is largest at that mean. Consequently, every doubly stochastic matrix with at most two distinct columns satisfies the Gram contraction inequality.
3. Singleton coefficient extraction, followed by capacity balancing or a capacity limit, preserves the rank-two matrix-product class.
4. For a rank-two nonnegative matrix, a column containing a zero has a real-rooted collision marginal. The collision law is not generally balanced, and a proposed Fock-capacity shortcut to repair that mismatch is false.

All proofs are mathematical derivations checked during the AI-assisted investigation, not a formal proof-assistant verification or human specialist review. No novelty claim is made.

## 1. Rank-two matrices and the binomial noise operator

Put
\[
c_n=\frac{n!}{n^n},\qquad
p_A(x)=\prod_{i=1}^n(Ax)_i,\qquad
N(p)=\sum_\alpha \alpha!p_\alpha^2.
\]
The permanent Gram identity gives
\[
N(p_A)=\operatorname{per}(AA^T),\qquad
[x_1\cdots x_n]p_A=\operatorname{per}(A).
\]

Every nonconstant doubly stochastic matrix of rank at most two has a representation
\[
A_{ij}=\frac{x_i y_j}{t}
+\frac{(1-x_i)(1-y_j)}{n-t},
\quad
x,y\in[0,1]^n,\quad \sum_i x_i=\sum_j y_j=t\in(0,n).
\tag{1}
\]
Indeed, the rows lie on a line in the probability simplex. Choose the two endpoints of their convex hull, write the rows as convex combinations with coefficients \(x_i\), and set \(t=\sum x_i\). The column sums then give the indicated vector \(y\). Conversely, every matrix (1) is nonnegative, doubly stochastic, and of rank at most two.

Let
\[
\theta=t/n,\quad \sigma=\theta(1-\theta),\quad
B=\operatorname{Bin}(n,\theta).
\]
For \(x\in[0,1]^n\), let \(P_x\) be the law of the sum of independent Bernoulli variables with parameters \(x_1,\ldots,x_n\). Set
\[
r=P_x/B,\qquad s=P_y/B,\qquad
\langle f,g\rangle_B=\sum_{k=0}^n B(k)f(k)g(k).
\]

Let \(T_\kappa\), \(0\le\kappa\le1\), be the count version of the following noise operation on independent Bernoulli(\(\theta\)) bits: keep a bit with probability \(\kappa\), and otherwise replace it by a fresh Bernoulli(\(\theta\)) bit. This is a self-adjoint positive operator in the \(B\)-weighted inner product. On likelihood ratios it sends \(P_x/B\) to \(P_{x'}/B\), where
\[
x'_i=\theta+\kappa(x_i-\theta).
\]

The exact identities from the round-4 note are
\[
\frac{\operatorname{per}(A)}{c_n}=\langle r,s\rangle_B,
\qquad
\frac{\operatorname{per}(AA^T)}{c_n}
=\langle r,T_{\kappa(s)}r\rangle_B,
\tag{2}
\]
where
\[
\kappa(s)=1-\frac{\operatorname{Var}(P_y)}{n\sigma}
=\frac{\sum_j(y_j-\theta)^2}{n\sigma}.
\tag{3}
\]
In particular \(0\le\kappa(s)\le1\).

Thus the strong rank-two inequality is exactly
\[
\boxed{\quad
\langle r,s\rangle_B^2
\ \ge\
\langle r,T_{\kappa(s)}r\rangle_B.
\quad}
\tag{4}
\]
The strictly stronger proposed Gram contraction is
\[
\boxed{\quad
\langle r,s\rangle_B
\ \ge\
\langle r,T_{\kappa(s)}r\rangle_B,
\quad}
\tag{5}
\]
equivalently \(\operatorname{per}(AA^T)\le\operatorname{per}(A)\).

If (5) held for every rank-two doubly stochastic matrix, the van der Waerden bound \(\operatorname{per}(A)\ge c_n\) would imply (4). Formula (5) is false for unrestricted matrices: for \(A=(I+P_4)/2\), one has \(\operatorname{per}(A)=1/8\) and \(\operatorname{per}(AA^T)=9/64\). Its validity for all rank-two matrices remains unproved in this work.

### Separate convexity

The orthonormal Krawtchouk expansion of a likelihood ratio of mass one and mean \(t\) has the form
\[
r=1+\sum_{k=2}^n a_k K_k,
\qquad T_\kappa K_k=\kappa^k K_k.
\]
There is no degree-one term because the means agree. Consequently
\[
\langle r,T_\kappa r\rangle_B
=1+\sum_{k=2}^n a_k^2\kappa^k.
\tag{6}
\]

Consider the deficit
\[
D(r,s)=
\sqrt{\langle r,T_{\kappa(s)}r\rangle_B}
-\langle r,s\rangle_B.
\tag{7}
\]
For fixed \(s\), this is a Hilbert norm minus a linear functional of \(r\), and is convex. For fixed \(r\), its norm term is
\[
\left\|(1,\ |a_2|\kappa,\ |a_3|\kappa^{3/2},\ldots,
|a_n|\kappa^{n/2})\right\|_2.
\]
All component functions are nonnegative and convex for \(\kappa\ge0\), and the Euclidean norm is convex and nondecreasing in each nonnegative coordinate. The displayed function is therefore convex in \(\kappa\). Since the variance is linear in the law when its mean is fixed, (3) is affine in \(s\), proving convexity in the second argument as well.

The stronger contraction deficit
\[
E(r,s)=\langle r,T_{\kappa(s)}r\rangle_B-\langle r,s\rangle_B
\tag{8}
\]
is likewise separately convex: it is a positive quadratic form in \(r\), and a sum of nonnegative multiples of \(\kappa^k\), minus a linear functional, in \(s\).

## 2. Exact localization within the Poisson-binomial class

The linear-functional version of the extremal reduction below is classical, due to Hoeffding (1956), Corollary 2.1: [primary publication](https://projecteuclid.org/journals/annals-of-mathematical-statistics/volume-27/issue-3/On-the-Distribution-of-the-Number-of-Successes-in-Independent/10.1214/aoms/1177728178.full). See also Section 2 of [Pitman's author paper](https://statistics.berkeley.edu/sites/default/files/tech-reports/453.pdf). The following argument supplies the convex-functional version used here, with a tie-break that ensures an extremizer of the stated form. No novelty claim is made.

### Lemma

Fix \(n\) and \(t\in[0,n]\). Every continuous convex functional of a Poisson-binomial probability law with mean \(t\) has a maximizer whose Bernoulli parameter vector takes values in
\[
\{0,p,1\}
\]
for a single \(p\in(0,1)\). Thus a maximizer has the law
\[
P=a+\operatorname{Bin}\!\left(m,\frac{t-a}{m}\right),
\quad
a,m\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0},\quad a+m\le n,\quad a<t<a+m.
\tag{9}
\]
A deterministic law is included when \(t\) is an integer.

### Proof

The parameter polytope \(\{x\in[0,1]^n:\sum x_i=t\}\) is compact. Among all maximizers choose one maximizing \(\sum_i x_i^2\).

Suppose two parameters \(x_i,x_j\) are distinct and both strictly between zero and one. Keep their sum \(u=x_i+x_j\) fixed, and write \(z=x_i x_j\). Their contribution to the probability generating polynomial is
\[
(1-x_i+x_i w)(1-x_j+x_j w)
=(1-u)+uw+z(1-w)^2.
\tag{10}
\]
The full law is therefore affine in \(z\), while the chosen objective is convex in \(z\).

For two distinct interior parameters, \(z\) lies strictly inside its feasible interval: the upper endpoint makes the two parameters equal, and the lower endpoint puts at least one at zero or one. A convex function attaining its global maximum at an interior point has both endpoint values equal to that maximum. Choosing the lower endpoint increases
\[
x_i^2+x_j^2=u^2-2z
\]
without decreasing the objective. This contradicts the choice of the maximizer. All interior parameters must therefore coincide. This proves (9).

### Consequence for the rank-two problem

For fixed \(n,t\), take a global maximizer of (7) over the two Poisson-binomial laws. Apply the lemma first to one law and then to the other, using separate convexity. Both replacement steps preserve global maximality. Hence (4) for all rank-two matrices is equivalent to (4) for pairs of shifted binomial laws (9). Exactly the same statement holds for the stronger candidate (5), using (8).

This is a genuine reduction within the matrix class. It does not replace Poisson-binomial laws by arbitrary ultra-log-concave laws, and it does not prove the remaining inequality.

## 3. Integer-mean likelihood peak: a proved scalar theorem

### Theorem

Let \(P\) be a Poisson-binomial law on \(\{0,\ldots,n\}\) with integer mean \(h\), where \(0<h<n\). Let \(B=\operatorname{Bin}(n,h/n)\). Then
\[
\boxed{\qquad
\frac{P(k)}{B(k)}\le \frac{P(h)}{B(h)}
\quad (0\le k\le n).
\qquad}
\tag{11}
\]

### Proof

Fix \(k\). The functional \(P(k)/B(k)-P(h)/B(h)\) is linear in \(P\). By the localization lemma, its maximum occurs at
\[
P=a+\operatorname{Bin}(\ell,p),
\quad p=(h-a)/\ell.
\]
It suffices to prove (11) for such a law.

Put \(r=h-a\) and \(s=\ell-r\). The deterministic cases are immediate. Otherwise \(r,s>0\), and the likelihood-ratio sequence is log-concave on its interval of support. One can check this without any root theorem: up to a positive constant and a geometric factor, its \(j\)th term is
\[
\frac{\binom{\ell}{j-a}}{\binom{n}{j}}
=\frac{(j)_a(n-j)_{n-a-\ell}}{(n)_{n-\ell}},
\tag{12}
\]
where \((z)_b=z(z-1)\cdots(z-b+1)\). Each falling-factorial factor is log-concave on its positive support, and multiplication by a geometric factor preserves log-concavity.

At the mean, direct binomial ratios give
\[
\frac{P(h+1)/B(h+1)}{P(h)/B(h)}
=\frac{r(h+1)}{h(r+1)}\le1
\]
because \(r\le h\), and
\[
\frac{P(h-1)/B(h-1)}{P(h)/B(h)}
=\frac{s(n-h+1)}{(n-h)(s+1)}\le1
\]
because \(s\le n-h\). Terms outside the support are zero. A log-concave sequence whose two neighbors do not exceed its central term has its global maximum there. This proves the theorem.

### Collision-norm consequence

Writing \(r=P/B\), (11) gives
\[
\sum_k\frac{P(k)^2}{B(k)}
=\mathbb E_P r
\le r(h).
\tag{13}
\]
Also \(r(h)\ge1\), since \(\mathbb E_Br=1\). Therefore
\[
\sum_k\frac{P(k)^2}{B(k)}
\le r(h)\le r(h)^2.
\tag{14}
\]

### Matrix consequence: at most two distinct columns

Let \(A\) have \(h\) identical columns of one type and \(n-h\) identical columns of the other. Write their entries as
\[
A_{ij}=
\begin{cases}
x_i/h,&j\text{ in the first group},\\
(1-x_i)/(n-h),&j\text{ in the second group},
\end{cases}
\quad
x_i\in[0,1],\quad \sum_i x_i=h.
\tag{15}
\]
The corresponding second law is the deterministic law at \(h\), so (2) gives
\[
\operatorname{per}(A)/c_n=r(h),
\qquad
\operatorname{per}(AA^T)/c_n=\sum_kP_x(k)^2/B(k).
\]
Thus (13) proves
\[
\boxed{\operatorname{per}(AA^T)\le\operatorname{per}(A)}
\tag{16}
\]
for every such matrix, in every order. In particular, the strong Gram inequality holds for this family.

Transposition gives the other Gram comparison for a matrix with at most two distinct rows. If a matrix has at most two distinct rows and at most two distinct columns, both Gram comparisons hold, and permanent Cauchy--Schwarz yields original Gyires. A general rank-two matrix can have many distinct rows and columns; (16) does not cover that case.

## 4. The explicit one-parameter inequality which remains

For the localized laws take parameter vectors
\[
x=(1^a,q^m,0^b),\quad
y=(1^c,r^\ell,0^d),
\quad a+m+b=c+\ell+d=n,
\]
where
\[
q=(t-a)/m,\qquad r=(t-c)/\ell,
\qquad
\max(a,c)\le t\le\min(a+m,c+\ell).
\tag{17}
\]
Here \(m,\ell>0\); endpoint degeneracies are taken by continuity.

With row multiplicities \(a,m,b\) and column multiplicities \(c,\ell,d\), the matrix (1) has the block values
\[
\begin{pmatrix}
\displaystyle\frac1t&
\displaystyle\frac{t-c}{\ell t}&0\\[2mm]
\displaystyle\frac{t-a}{mt}&
\displaystyle\frac{m+\ell-n+ac/t+bd/(n-t)}{m\ell}&
\displaystyle\frac{n-b-t}{m(n-t)}\\[2mm]
0&
\displaystyle\frac{n-d-t}{\ell(n-t)}&
\displaystyle\frac1{n-t}
\end{pmatrix}.
\tag{18}
\]
Empty row or column groups are simply omitted.

For a fully algebraic version put
\[
u_k=e_k(x-\theta\mathbf1),\quad
v_k=e_k(y-\theta\mathbf1),\quad
\kappa_y=1-\frac{\ell r(1-r)}{n\sigma}.
\]
Then
\[
\langle r,s\rangle_B
=\sum_{k=0}^n\frac{u_kv_k}{\binom nk\sigma^k},
\qquad
\langle r,T_{\kappa_y}r\rangle_B
=\sum_{k=0}^n\frac{u_k^2\kappa_y^k}{\binom nk\sigma^k}.
\tag{19}
\]
The zeroth terms are one and the first terms are zero.

The stronger contraction question is precisely
\[
\boxed{\quad
\sum_{k=2}^n
\frac{u_kv_k-u_k^2\kappa_y^k}{\binom nk\sigma^k}\ge0
\quad}
\tag{20}
\]
for all admissible integer multiplicities and all \(t\) in (17). The strong Gyires question instead squares the first sum in (19). For fixed multiplicities, denominators can be cleared with positive constants and \([t(n-t)]^{2n}\), producing a univariate polynomial of degree at most \(4n\). This does not supply a uniform sign proof: (20), and the weaker squared version, are still open in this investigation.

### An endpoint collision problem

Even the orientation opposite to (16) leaves a useful explicit question. Suppose \(P_x=\delta_h\), while
\[
P_y=c+\operatorname{Bin}(\ell,(h-c)/\ell).
\]
Put
\[
r=h-c,\quad s=\ell-r,\quad v=\frac{rs}{r+s},\quad N=n-h.
\]
When \(r,s>0\), contraction is equivalent to
\[
\Pr\!\left[
\operatorname{Bin}(h,v/h)=\operatorname{Bin}(N,v/N)
\right]
\le
\binom{r+s}{r}
\left(\frac{r}{r+s}\right)^r
\left(\frac{s}{r+s}\right)^s,
\quad h\ge r,\quad N\ge s.
\tag{21}
\]
The two binomial variables on the left are independent. The right side is the same collision probability at the corner \(h=r,N=s\).

Both edges of this quadrant are proved. Set \(\gamma=s/(r+s)\), take independent
\[
R\sim\operatorname{Bin}(h,r/h),\quad
S\sim\operatorname{Bin}(N,s/N),
\]
and then thin \(R\) by probability \(\gamma\), and \(S\) by probability \(1-\gamma\). The two thinned counts have the laws on the left of (21). Given \(R,S\), their collision probability is
\[
H(R,S)=\binom{R+S}{R}(1-\gamma)^R\gamma^S.
\]
If \(h=r\), then \(R=r\) deterministically, and
\[
\frac{H(r,S+1)}{H(r,S)}
=\gamma\frac{r+S+1}{S+1}.
\]
Thus its maximum occurs at \(S=s-1,s\), and averaging gives (21). The other edge follows symmetrically.

The interior \(h>r,N>s\) remains unproved. Separate monotonicity of the collision probability in the two trial counts would be too strong: for \(r=s=1\), the equal-count collision is \(0.4609375\) at \(h=N=2\), while its limit as \(h=N\to\infty\) is \(e^{-1}I_0(1)\), approximately \(0.46576\). This increase still stays below the corner value \(1/2\), so it is an obstruction to that monotonicity argument, not a counterexample to (21).


## 5. Exact obstructions to several stronger shortcuts

These examples refute auxiliary assertions, not Gyires's conjecture.

### 5.1 Contraction does not extend to all ultra-log-concave laws

Take \(n=4,t=2\), let \(B=\operatorname{Bin}(4,1/2)\), and set
\[
P=\delta_2,\qquad Q=(0,2/7,3/7,2/7,0).
\]
Both likelihood ratios relative to \(B\) are log-concave, and both laws have mean two. The second is \(B\) conditioned on \(\{1,2,3\}\). Nevertheless
\[
\langle P/B,Q/B\rangle_B=8/7,\qquad \kappa(Q/B)=3/7,
\]
while
\[
\langle P/B,T_{3/7}(P/B)\rangle_B
=1+\frac23(3/7)^2+(3/7)^4
=\frac{2776}{2401}
=\frac87+\frac{32}{2401}.
\]
Thus contraction (5) fails for the broader ultra-log-concave class. The law \(Q\) is not Poisson-binomial: its generating polynomial is
\[
z(2+3z+2z^2)/7,
\]
whose quadratic factor has negative discriminant. The squared inequality (4) still holds in this example.

### 5.2 A noninteger interpolation of the likelihood peak fails

Take \(n=3,t=7/4\) and \(P=\operatorname{Bin}(2,7/8)\), padded by one zero Bernoulli parameter. Relative to \(B=\operatorname{Bin}(3,7/12)\), the likelihood ratio is
\[
r=(27/125,\ 18/25,\ 9/5,\ 0).
\]
Then
\[
\mathbb E_P r=1539/1000
>
\frac14r(1)+\frac34r(2)=153/100.
\]
Therefore replacing an integer mean by linear interpolation between its two neighbors does not preserve the collision-norm bound (13).

### 5.3 Convex order does not provide the needed relative log-concavity

In (1), noise sends \(x\) to \(x'=Ay\), so \(x'\) is majorized by \(y\). This does put the corresponding Poisson-binomial laws in a useful convex-order relation, but does not give the desired inequality for a general likelihood ratio.

A specific stronger candidate, relative log-concavity of \(P_y/P_{x'}\), is false. Take
\[
n=3,\quad t=5/4,\quad
x=(5/8,5/8,0),\quad y=(1,1/8,1/8).
\]
Here \(\kappa_y=7/10\), so
\[
x'=(9/16,9/16,1/8),
\]
and
\[
P_{x'}=(343,931,693,81)/2048,\quad
P_y=(0,49/64,7/32,1/64).
\]
The ratio at indices \(1,2,3\) is
\[
(1568/931,\ 64/99,\ 32/81).
\]
Its middle log-concavity ratio is
\[
\frac{(64/99)^2}{(1568/931)(32/81)}=\frac{76}{121}<1.
\]

### 5.4 The real-mean interval cannot be reduced to its endpoints

For \(n=3\), take both profiles \(a=c=1,m=\ell=1\), with \(1\le t\le2\). At either endpoint the two laws are the same deterministic law, and
\[
D=\sqrt{9/4}-9/4=-3/4.
\]
At \(t=3/2\), both likelihood ratios are \((0,4/3,4/3,0)\), and
\[
D=\sqrt{31/27}-4/3>-3/4.
\]
Thus the maximum of the deficit can occur away from the endpoints of its allowed mean interval. The inequality itself is not violated.

### 5.5 Arbitrary column averaging can increase a rank-two permanent

The exact example in the accompanying boundary note is
\[
A=\frac1{30}
\begin{pmatrix}4&15&11\\4&15&11\\22&0&8\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\widetilde A=\frac1{30}
\begin{pmatrix}4&13&13\\4&13&13\\22&4&4\end{pmatrix}.
\]
The second matrix averages columns two and three, but
\[
\operatorname{per}(A)=137/450,\qquad
\operatorname{per}(\widetilde A)=689/2250,
\qquad
\operatorname{per}(\widetilde A)-\operatorname{per}(A)=2/1125>0.
\]
Both matrices are doubly stochastic of rank two. This prevents a proof of (5) based on the assertion that every column mixing decreases the permanent.

The same matrix also has
\[
\operatorname{per}(A_{[1,2,2]})=11/30>137/450,
\]
where the second column is repeated and the third omitted. Thus the scalar likelihood-peak theorem does not extend to a pointwise maximum over all multivariate occupancies, even for rank two.

## 6. Singleton deletion is closed within rank two, including capacity limits

A full independent derivation is given in [round5_rank2_closure_audit.md](round5_rank2_closure_audit.md). The essential facts are recorded here because they make a genuine induction possible if one further norm estimate can be proved.

### Algebraic closure

Write a rank-two row product as
\[
p(x)=F(L_1(x),L_2(x)),\qquad
F(u,v)=\prod_i(\alpha_i u+\beta_i v),
\]
where all coefficients are nonnegative. If
\[
L_1=a x_j+L'_1,\qquad L_2=b x_j+L'_2,
\]
then
\[
q_j=[x_j]p
=(a\partial_u+b\partial_v)F(L'_1,L'_2).
\tag{22}
\]
A nonnegative directional derivative of the binary real-rooted product \(F\) is again a product of nonnegative binary linear forms, unless it is zero. An elementary proof takes distinct negative roots, observes alternating signs of the positive sum of omitted-factor products at successive roots, and then uses limits for repeated roots or factors with a zero coefficient. Substitution into (22) therefore gives a product matrix of rank at most two.

For a doubly stochastic \(A\), the central coefficient of \(q_j\) is \(\operatorname{per}(A)>0\); Hall's theorem, applied to positive entries, proves the latter directly. Thus the zero-polynomial exception is absent.

### A unique capacity-balanced limit

For a homogeneous degree-\(m\) polynomial \(q\) in \(m\) variables with nonnegative coefficients and positive central coefficient, define
\[
\gamma=\operatorname{cap}(q)
=\inf_{x>0}\frac{q(x)}{x_1\cdots x_m}>0.
\]
Use determinant-one scales \(z\in\mathbb R^m\), \(\sum z_i=0\), and write
\[
q_z(x)=q(e^{z_1}x_1,\ldots,e^{z_m}x_m),\qquad C(z)=q_z(\mathbf1).
\]
The identity
\[
\sum_\alpha
\left(\sqrt{q_\alpha e^{\alpha\cdot z}}
-\sqrt{q_\alpha e^{\alpha\cdot w}}\right)^2
=C(z)+C(w)-2C((z+w)/2)
\le C(z)+C(w)-2\gamma
\tag{23}
\]
shows that every capacity-minimizing sequence has the same coefficientwise limit \(\bar q\). This limit satisfies
\[
\bar q(\mathbf1)=\gamma,\qquad
\bar q_{\mathbf1}=q_{\mathbf1},\qquad
\partial_i\bar q(\mathbf1)=\gamma.
\tag{24}
\]
For the last assertion, pass the capacity inequality to the limit and differentiate its minimum at the all-ones point.

Define the balanced deletion norm by
\[
H(q)=N(\bar q).
\tag{25}
\]
This is unambiguous even when no finite diagonal scale attains the capacity.

If \(q\) is a rank-two product, row-normalizing its scaled factor matrices gives a compact family of row-stochastic matrices of rank at most two. Every convergent subsequence represents \(\bar q/\gamma\). Balance in (24) makes the limiting column sums one. Consequently
\[
\bar q/\gamma=p_M
\]
for a doubly stochastic matrix \(M\) of rank at most two.

### The exact missing deletion inequality

The row-pair boundary reduction in [round5_matrix_boundary.md](round5_matrix_boundary.md) stays inside the rank-two class. Combining it with the closure above gives the following conditional proof strategy.

It would suffice to prove that, for every doubly stochastic rank-two matrix \(A\) with a zero entry, at least one column \(j\) containing a zero satisfies
\[
\boxed{\qquad
H([x_j]p_A)\ge g_nN(p_A),
\qquad
g_n=\left(\frac{n-1}{n}\right)^{n-1}.
\qquad}
\tag{26}
\]
Indeed, the rank-two induction hypothesis at order \(n-1\), applied to \(\bar q_j/\gamma\), would give
\[
\operatorname{per}(A)^2
\ge c_{n-1}H(q_j)
\ge c_{n-1}g_nN(p_A)
=c_nN(p_A).
\tag{27}
\]
The central coefficient is preserved by (24), and the normalization factor \(\gamma\) cancels correctly when the inductive inequality is multiplied back by \(\gamma^2\).

**Equation (26) is unproved.** Algebraic closure and the existence of the balanced limit do not imply it. Requiring the same inequality for every column is too strong: rank-two counterexamples occur already in order three for columns without a zero. The zero-containing-column condition is therefore substantive.

For an exact counterexample to the every-column version, take
\[
A=\frac1{1500}
\begin{pmatrix}
482&725&293\\
478&775&247\\
540&0&960
\end{pmatrix}.
\]
This matrix is doubly stochastic of rank two, and its only zero is in column two. For deletion of the first column,
\[
q=[x_1]p_A=a y^2+b yz+c z^2,\qquad
a=\frac{899}{10000},\quad
b=\frac{303539}{1125000},\quad
c=\frac{4797067}{56250000}.
\]
Balancing the two remaining variables gives \(H(q)=b^2+4ac\), and direct rational arithmetic yields
\[
g_3N(p_A)-H(q)
=\frac{4550848849393}{1186523437500000}>0,
\qquad
\frac{H(q)}{g_3N(p_A)}
=\frac{122764681516875}{127315530366268}<1.
\]
The failed column contains no zero, so this does not refute (26).

## 7. A zero-column collision marginal is real-rooted

### Theorem

Let \(A\ge0\) have rank at most two, with no zero rows. Suppose a nonzero column \(a=a_j\) contains a zero entry. Then
\[
h_j(z)=\operatorname{per}(AA^T+(z-1)aa^T)
\tag{28}
\]
has nonnegative coefficients and only nonpositive real roots. A positive constant polynomial is allowed.

If \(q_k=[x_j^k]p_A\), then
\[
h_j(z)=\sum_{k=0}^n k!N(q_k)z^k.
\tag{29}
\]
Thus (28) is the probability generating polynomial, before normalization, of the column occupancy under the Fock collision weights.

### Proof

The hypotheses exclude rank one: in a nonnegative rank-one matrix with no zero rows, every nonzero column is strictly positive. Hence write
\[
G=AA^T=UU^T
\]
with \(U\) of full column rank two. The vector \(a\) belongs to the column space of \(U\), so \(a=Uw\) for some \(w\ne0\). Since
\[
G-aa^T=A_{-j}A_{-j}^T\ge0,
\]
full column rank implies \(I-ww^T\ge0\), and therefore
\[
0<s:=\|w\|^2\le1.
\tag{30}
\]

Choose a row \(i\) with \(a_i=0\). Its feature vector \(u_i\ne0\) is orthogonal to \(w\). Choose the orthonormal basis
\[
e_1=u_i/\|u_i\|,\qquad e_2=w/\|w\|.
\]
Let \((\alpha_k,\beta_k)\) be the coordinates of the \(k\)th row of \(U\) in this basis. Both are nonnegative:
\[
\alpha_k=G_{ki}/\|u_i\|\ge0,\qquad
\beta_k=a_k/\|w\|\ge0.
\tag{31}
\]
Here \(G_{ki}\ge0\) because \(A\ge0\). In these coordinates,
\[
G+(z-1)aa^T
=\alpha\alpha^T+(1-s+sz)\beta\beta^T.
\tag{32}
\]

Put
\[
f(t)=\prod_{k=1}^n(\alpha_k+\beta_k t)
=\sum_{k=0}^n f_k t^k.
\]
It has only nonpositive real roots; zero coefficients or a missing top degree are permitted. The rank-two permanent expansion gives
\[
h_j(z)=\sum_{k=0}^n k!(n-k)!\,f_k^2(1-s+sz)^k.
\tag{33}
\]
For example, this identity follows by expanding the binary product and applying the Fock Gram formula in two variables.

The Schur--Szegő composition theorem says that polynomials with all roots negative are closed under binomially normalized coefficientwise composition. In particular,
\[
R(t)=\sum_{k=0}^n\frac{f_k^2}{\binom nk}t^k
\]
has only nonpositive real roots. Zeros and missing degree are obtained as limits of strictly negative-rooted degree-\(n\) polynomials. A primary source is Kostov and Shapiro, *On the Schur--Szegő composition of polynomials*, C. R. Acad. Sci. Paris 343 (2006), 81--86, Proposition 1.5:
https://comptes-rendus.academie-sciences.fr/mathematique/item/10.1016/j.crma.2006.06.007.pdf

An accessible copy of the same published article is https://www.numdam.org/item/10.1016/j.crma.2006.06.007.pdf.

By (33), \(h_j(z)=n!R(1-s+sz)\). Since \(s>0\) and \(1-s\ge0\), every root is nonpositive. Formula (29), obtained by scaling \(x_j\) by \(\sqrt z\) in the Fock norm, supplies coefficient nonnegativity directly.

The same argument applies after arbitrary positive scaling of the other columns, since rank two and the zero entry are preserved. No doubly stochastic hypothesis is needed for the theorem itself.

### The collision law is not generally balanced

Take
\[
A=
\begin{pmatrix}
1/2&1/4&1/4\\
1/2&1/4&1/4\\
0&1/2&1/2
\end{pmatrix}.
\]
This is doubly stochastic of rank two, and its first-column collision polynomial is
\[
h_1(z)=\frac{3+8z+4z^2}{64}.
\tag{34}
\]
Its roots are \(-1/2,-3/2\), as the theorem predicts. However,
\[
h_1'(1)/h_1(1)=16/15\ne1.
\]
The normalized collision law is a sum of Bernoulli variables with parameters \(2/3,2/5\), whose mean is \(16/15\). The integer-mean likelihood theorem cannot therefore be applied to it with target mean one. Real-rootedness alone does not close (26).


For completeness, a zero row makes \(h_j\) identically zero; in that degenerate case there is no normalized collision law. A zero column makes \(h_j\) a constant. In rank one with no zero rows, only this latter column case can occur. These degeneracies do not arise for a zero-containing nonzero column of a doubly stochastic matrix.

## 8. A Fock-capacity repair is false

The lack of mean balance in (34) suggested replacing the Fock norm by its diagonal-scaling capacity,
\[
\mathcal C_F(p)=\inf_{D>0}\frac{N(p(Dx))}{(\det D)^2}.
\tag{35}
\]
A standard one-variable real-rooted coefficient-versus-capacity estimate, applied to the collision marginal, would make the following comparison sufficient for (26):
\[
\mathcal C_F(p)/N(p)\ge g_n/g_d,
\tag{36}
\]
where \(d\) is the number of positive entries in the chosen column. Since the column contains a zero, \(d\le n-1\). The convention \(g_1=1\) covers degree one.

**Comparison (36) is false, even in the already proved one-special-column family.** The exact finite certificate is in [round5_fock_capacity_obstruction.md](round5_fock_capacity_obstruction.md) and its accompanying rational-arithmetic script.

Take \(n=50\),
\[
u=(0,1/2,\underbrace{1/96,\ldots,1/96}_{48}),
\qquad
A_{i1}=u_i,\quad A_{ij}=(1-u_i)/49\ (j\ge2).
\]
This matrix is doubly stochastic of rank two, with exactly one zero entry, so \(d=49\). Put
\[
P=\operatorname{Bern}(1/2)+\operatorname{Bin}(48,1/96),\qquad
B=\operatorname{Bin}(50,1/50),\qquad
C(z)=\sum_k\frac{P(k)^2}{B(k)}z^k.
\]
Scaling only the first variable by \(\sqrt z\) gives
\[
\frac{\mathcal C_F(p_A)}{N(p_A)}
\le\frac{C(z)}{zC(1)}.
\]
At the rational value \(z=31/32\), exact arithmetic proves
\[
\frac{C(31/32)}{(31/32)C(1)}
<\frac{99973}{100000}
<\frac{99979}{100000}
<\frac{g_{50}}{g_{49}}.
\tag{37}
\]
Thus a proof of (26) must retain more information than \(\mathcal C_F(p_A)\) alone. Equation (37) does not refute (26), the strong Gram inequality, or original Gyires.

## 9. Reproducible diagnostics and their limits

The mathematical proofs above do not depend on numerical searches. The searches were used to test precise candidate implications and to identify possible counterexamples. They are recorded so that the distinction between a reduction and a proof is inspectable.

- [round5_rank2_profiles.py](round5_rank2_profiles.py) and its JSON output evaluate the exact scalar formulas for shifted-binomial profiles. They also exhibit the failure of mean-endpoint maximization.
- [round5_rank2_profile_min.cpp](round5_rank2_profile_min.cpp) tests the stronger contraction deficit on ordered profile pairs. The accompanying log records the completed targeted search through orders 16--22. The search includes points close to each endpoint, points within each unit mean interval, and local one-dimensional refinements. It found no negative deficit, but uses floating-point arithmetic and does not certify all roots, all means, or all orders.
- [round5_rank2_closure_audit.md](round5_rank2_closure_audit.md) contains the independent self-contained proof of the algebraic and capacity-limit closure needed by the proposed induction.
- [round5_fock_capacity_obstruction.py](round5_fock_capacity_obstruction.py) verifies the strict rational inequalities in (37).

The order-16--22 profile search covered 175,574 ordered profile pairs and logged 5,723,130 initial trial means, cumulatively, in addition to its local refinements. These counts are a description of the diagnostic run, not evidence of a general theorem. Identical profiles and full-binomial profiles were omitted from that run because their contraction inequalities follow immediately from the positive noise spectrum or give equality.

For a direct reproduction of the targeted search, compile the C++ source with an optimizing C++17 compiler and run the resulting executable in the same directory. Its order range and deterministic trial-point schedule are in the source. Running the Python scripts uses the dependencies listed by their imports; the Fock-capacity certificate uses exact rational arithmetic.

## 10. What is established and what is still required

The complete implications proved here are
\[
\begin{aligned}
&\text{rank-two doubly stochastic matrices}\\
&\qquad\Longleftrightarrow
\text{the latent two-component representation (1)},\\[1mm]
&\text{strong rank-two inequality}\\
&\qquad\Longleftrightarrow
\text{the shifted-binomial-pair instance of (4)},\\[1mm]
&\text{rank-two boundary reduction and singleton closure}\\
&\qquad+\ \text{the unproved zero-column estimate (26)}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\text{strong rank-two inequality}.
\end{aligned}
\]

The likelihood-peak theorem proves Gram contraction when there are at most two distinct columns. The collision-marginal theorem gives real-rootedness for any zero-containing column in rank two. These are proved family and structural results. Neither one removes the gap in (20) or (26).

A successful continuation needs a uniform proof of the shifted-binomial-pair inequality (at least its squared version), or a proof of the balanced-deletion norm estimate (26), or a genuinely different argument. The all-rank-two case and the unrestricted original Gyires conjecture remain unresolved by this work.
