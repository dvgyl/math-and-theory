# Rank-two singleton deletion and capacity limits: independent audit

## Conclusion

The rank-two product class is closed under extracting one copy of a variable, except for the possible zero polynomial. For a balanced matrix product, that zero case cannot occur. Positive diagonal variable scaling preserves the class. If a capacity-balancing scale is not attained at a finite point, there is nevertheless a **unique coefficientwise balanced limit**, preserving the central coefficient. After normalization, that limit is the product polynomial of a doubly stochastic matrix of rank at most two.

The proof below is self-contained. In particular, it does not assume finite Sinkhorn scaling or cite an unproved capacity-limit assertion. It establishes closure, not the still-missing deletion norm inequality. This is an independent AI mathematical audit, not human review or proof-assistant verification. No novelty claim is made.

## 1. Every stochastic rank-two product has the required binary representation

Let $A$ be a row-stochastic nonnegative matrix of rank at most two. Its rows lie in a linear space of dimension at most two and in the affine hyperplane whose coordinates sum to one. Hence their affine span has dimension at most one.

If the rows are not all equal, choose the two endpoint rows $v_1,v_2$ of their convex hull. Every row is

\[
A_{i\bullet}=\theta_i v_1+(1-\theta_i)v_2,
\qquad 0\le\theta_i\le1.
\]

The nonnegative linear forms $L_1(x)=v_1\cdot x$, $L_2(x)=v_2\cdot x$ therefore give

\[
p_A(x)=F(L_1(x),L_2(x)),
\qquad
F(u,v)=\prod_{i=1}^n(\theta_i u+(1-\theta_i)v).
\tag{1}
\]

If all rows coincide, take $L_1=L_2$. This covers rank one without any separate nonnegative-factorization assumption.

More generally, the following argument applies whenever $p=F(L_1,L_2)$, with $F$ a product of nonnegative binary linear forms and $L_1,L_2$ nonnegative.

## 2. Singleton extraction remains a rank-two product

Fix a variable $x_j$ and write

\[
L_1=a x_j+L'_1,\qquad L_2=b x_j+L'_2,
\qquad a,b\ge0,
\]

where $L'_1,L'_2$ use only the remaining variables. The coefficient of exactly one copy of $x_j$ is

\[
q_j=[x_j]p
=\bigl(a\partial_u+b\partial_v\bigr)F(L'_1,L'_2).
\tag{2}
\]

The homogeneous binary polynomial

\[
G(u,v)=(a\partial_u+b\partial_v)F(u,v)
\]

is either zero or factors into nonnegative real linear forms. The following elementary proof checks that assertion, including degenerate factors.

First suppose every factor $c_i u+d_i v$ has $c_i,d_i>0$, the ratios $-d_i/c_i$ are distinct, and $(a,b)\ne(0,0)$. At $v=1$, the derivative is a positive linear combination of the polynomials obtained by omitting one factor:

\[
G(t,1)=\sum_i (ac_i+bd_i)
\prod_{k\ne i}(c_k t+d_k).
\]

Write the roots of $F(t,1)$ in increasing order as $r_1<\cdots<r_n<0$. At $r_i$, only the $i$th summand survives, with sign $(-1)^{n-i}$. The signs therefore alternate. The degree of $G(t,1)$ is $n-1$, so it has one root in each interval $(r_i,r_{i+1})$, and all its roots are real and negative. Its leading coefficient is positive, giving a factorization into nonnegative linear forms.

For repeated roots or zero coefficients, approximate the original coefficients by strictly positive generic coefficients. Normalize each binary factor of $G$ to have coefficient sum one. Those normalized coefficient pairs belong to the compact segment $\{(s,1-s):0\le s\le1\}$, while the remaining overall scalar is $G(1,1)$. If the limiting $G$ is nonzero, that scalar has a positive limit. A convergent subsequence of the factors thus gives a nonnegative factorization of the limiting homogeneous polynomial. This handles factors $u$ or $v$, including roots at zero or infinity. If $(a,b)=(0,0)$, or the derivative vanishes for another degenerate reason, $G=0$ is the stated exceptional case.

Substitute $L'_1,L'_2$ into the factorization of $G$. Each resulting factor is a nonnegative linear form in the remaining variables. Its row vector lies in the span of the two coefficient vectors of $L'_1,L'_2$. Therefore $q_j$ is either zero or the product polynomial of a nonnegative matrix of rank at most two.

If one restricted form vanishes or the two become linearly dependent, the rank may decrease; it cannot increase. If a substituted factor vanishes, the whole polynomial is zero, which is harmless in the general algebraic statement and excluded below in the matrix application.

## 3. Nonvanishing and positive capacity in the doubly stochastic case

For a doubly stochastic $A$, its permanent is positive. One elementary justification is Hall's condition: if a set of rows had fewer neighboring positive columns than rows, its total row mass would exceed the combined capacity of those columns, contradicting the column sums. A positive perfect matching therefore exists.

The central coefficient of every singleton deletion is unchanged:

\[
[\prod_{k\ne j}x_k]q_j=[x_1\cdots x_n]p_A
=\operatorname{per}(A)>0.
\tag{3}
\]

Consequently $q_j$ is nonzero. For any nonnegative homogeneous degree-$m$ polynomial $q$ in $m$ variables with central coefficient $d>0$, define

\[
\operatorname{cap}(q)=\inf_{x_i>0}
\frac{q(x)}{x_1\cdots x_m}.
\]

The central monomial gives

\[
0<d\le\operatorname{cap}(q)\le q(\mathbf1)<\infty.
\tag{4}
\]

Finite attainment should not be presumed for an arbitrary product polynomial. For example, $q(x,y)=x(x+y)$ has capacity one but no finite positive minimizer; the scales $(e^{-t},e^t)$ give $q_t=e^{-2t}x^2+xy\to xy$. The example is only to illustrate the general scaling issue; no claim is made that this particular polynomial is a singleton deletion of a balanced matrix product.

## 4. A unique balanced limit for every capacity-minimizing sequence

This argument holds for every nonnegative homogeneous polynomial of matching degree and number of variables with positive capacity, without a rank assumption.

By homogeneity, restrict variable scales to determinant one. Write $z\in\mathbb R^m$ with $\sum_i z_i=0$, and put

\[
q_z(x)=q(e^{z_1}x_1,\ldots,e^{z_m}x_m),
\qquad C(z)=q_z(\mathbf1),
\qquad \gamma=\operatorname{cap}(q)>0.
\]

Let $q_\alpha(z)=q_\alpha e^{\alpha\cdot z}$. For any two determinant-one scale vectors $z,w$,

\[
\begin{aligned}
\sum_\alpha\bigl(\sqrt{q_\alpha(z)}-
\sqrt{q_\alpha(w)}\bigr)^2
&=C(z)+C(w)-2C((z+w)/2)\\
&\le C(z)+C(w)-2\gamma.
\end{aligned}
\tag{5}
\]

Thus, along any capacity-minimizing sequence, the finite vector of square-root coefficients is Cauchy. Its coefficientwise limit $\bar q$ is unique, even across different minimizing sequences: apply (5) to one point from each sequence. We have

\[
\bar q(\mathbf1)=\gamma,
\qquad \bar q_{\mathbf1}=q_{\mathbf1}.
\tag{6}
\]

For any fixed $h\in\mathbb R^m$, the capacity definition gives

\[
\frac{q_z(e^{h_1},\ldots,e^{h_m})}{e^{\sum_i h_i}}
\ge\gamma.
\]

Pass to the coefficientwise limit. The smooth function
$\bar q(e^h)e^{-\sum_i h_i}$ attains its minimum $\gamma$ at $h=0$. Differentiating there yields

\[
\partial_i\bar q(\mathbf1)=\bar q(\mathbf1)=\gamma
\quad\text{for every }i.
\tag{7}
\]

Hence $\bar q$ is balanced. This proof uses neither finite attainment nor an external matrix-scaling convergence theorem.

Because the coefficient space is finite, the Fock norm is continuous. In particular,

\[
H(q):=N(\bar q)
=\lim_{k\to\infty}N(q_{z_k})
\tag{8}
\]

is well-defined and independent of the minimizing sequence. This supplies a precise definition of the capacity-balanced deletion norm at boundary points.

## 5. The balanced limit is still a rank-two matrix product

Now suppose $q$ is a product of $m$ nonnegative linear forms with coefficient matrix $B$ of rank at most two. Absorb any positive overall scalar into one row. Every row is nonzero because $q\ne0$.

For a positive diagonal scale $D_z$, normalize the rows of $B D_z$ to sum one, obtaining $M_z$. Its entries lie in $[0,1]$, and its rank remains at most two. Since the product of those row sums is $C(z)$,

\[
p_{M_z}(x)=\frac{q_z(x)}{C(z)}.
\tag{9}
\]

Along a capacity-minimizing sequence, take a convergent subsequence of the row-stochastic matrices $M_z$. Compactness gives a nonnegative row-stochastic limit $M$. Rank at most two is a closed condition, since all its $3\times3$ minors vanish. Equations (6) and (9) imply

\[
p_M=\frac{\bar q}{\gamma}.
\tag{10}
\]

The right side is balanced. For a row-stochastic matrix,

\[
\partial_jp_M(\mathbf1)=\sum_iM_{ij}.
\]

Balance therefore makes every column sum one. Thus $M$ is doubly stochastic and has rank at most two, proving the claimed closure through capacity limits.

The factor matrix limit need not be asserted unique; the polynomial limit and its norm are already unique by (5). That is all the induction would require.

The case $m=1$ is immediate. The original $n=1$ problem has a constant singleton deletion and can be treated separately as the trivial induction base.

## 6. Exact implication for a proposed rank-two induction

The direct row-pair boundary reduction in `round5_matrix_boundary.md` also stays inside the rank-at-most-two class: every varied row is a linear combination of the two original rows, so the row space cannot enlarge. The class of doubly stochastic matrices of rank at most two is compact.

Suppose one could prove the following missing statement for every rank-two boundary matrix of order $n$: at least one column $j$ containing a zero satisfies

\[
H(q_j)\ge g_nN(p_A),
\qquad g_n=\left(\frac{n-1}{n}\right)^{n-1}.
\tag{11}
\]

The closure proved above permits the induction hypothesis in order $n-1$ to be applied to $\bar q_j$. Since its central coefficient remains $\operatorname{per}(A)$,

\[
\operatorname{per}(A)^2
\ge c_{n-1}H(q_j)
\ge c_{n-1}g_nN(p_A)
=c_nN(p_A).
\]

Together with the boundary reduction, this would prove the strong rank-two inequality in every order. But **(11) is not proved here**. The closure and scaling arguments do not imply it, and numerical success does not replace this missing inequality.
