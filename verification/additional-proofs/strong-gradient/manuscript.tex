\begin{abstract}
Taylor, Hendrickx, and Glineur conjectured the exact final-gradient norm
of constant-step gradient descent on smooth strongly convex functions,
under a bound on the initial distance to a minimizer. An explicit
finite interpolation certificate for the balanced step, together with two reductions,
covers every normalized step in $[0,2]$, every finite horizon, and every
condition ratio in $[0,1]$. The certificate retains a joint terminal
potential. On the progress branch, scaling the objective preserves a
weighted interpolation surplus. On the oscillation branch, decreasing
an auxiliary curvature parameter, sometimes below zero, brings the
given step to balance. The scalar factors remain nonnegative throughout
this auxiliary extension. The familiar one-dimensional strongly convex
Huber spline and quadratic attain the two branches. The previously
established smooth-convex curve is prior work; the contribution is the
complete positive strong-curvature case. The actual-function upper bound
and normalized attainment, including every closed parameter boundary,
have been checked in Lean~4.19.0. The literature review is bounded by
the sources and versions cited here.
\end{abstract}

\section{Problem, statement, and prior work}

Let $E$ be a real Hilbert space, $L>0$, and $0\leq\mu\leq L$.
Let $f:E\to\R$ be differentiable with an $L$-Lipschitz gradient and
$\mu$-strongly convex:
\begin{align}
\norm{\grad f(x)-\grad f(y)}&\leq L\norm{x-y},\label{eq:smooth}\\
f(y)&\geq f(x)+\ip{\grad f(x)}{y-x}+\frac\mu2\norm{y-x}^2.
\label{eq:strong}
\end{align}
For differentiable functions, \eqref{eq:strong} is equivalent to
convexity of $x\mapsto f(x)-\mu\norm{x}^2/2$. Suppose $x_*$ is a
global minimizer and consider
\begin{equation}
x_{i+1}=x_i-\frac hL\grad f(x_i),\qquad 0\leq i<N,\qquad 0\leq h\leq2.
\label{eq:gd}
\end{equation}
Write $\kappa=\mu/L$ and $R=\norm{x_0-x_*}$. Define
\begin{equation}
p_N(\kappa,h)=
\begin{cases}
\displaystyle\frac1{1+Nh},&\kappa=0,\\[6pt]
\displaystyle\frac{\kappa(1-\kappa h)^N}
 {1-(1-\kappa)(1-\kappa h)^N},&0<\kappa\leq1,
\end{cases}
\quad
\rho_N(\kappa,h)=\max\{p_N(\kappa,h),\,|1-h|^N\}.
\label{eq:rate}
\end{equation}
For $\kappa>0$ the denominator is at least $\kappa$, since
$|1-\kappa h|\leq1$. Thus the formula includes $1-\kappa h=0$,
$h=0$, and $N=0$. Every zeroth power is one. The progress expression
may be negative when $1-\kappa h<0$ and $N$ is odd; the maximum is
nevertheless nonnegative.

\begin{theorem}[Exact last-gradient bound]
\label{thm:main}
Under \eqref{eq:smooth}--\eqref{eq:gd}, for every integer $N\geq0$,
\begin{equation}
\norm{\grad f(x_N)}\leq LR\,\rho_N(\mu/L,h).
\label{eq:main}
\end{equation}
For every admissible $\mu,L,h,N$ and $R>0$, equality is attained by
a one-dimensional function in the same class with initial distance $R$.
\end{theorem}

When $\kappa>0$ and $1-\kappa h\neq0$, the original conjectured
progress expression is
\[
\frac{\kappa}{(\kappa-1)+(1-\kappa h)^{-N}},
\]
which equals \eqref{eq:rate}. The rational form also specifies its
continuous extension through a zero base. Its limit at $\kappa=0$
is $1/(1+Nh)$: with $a=1-\kappa h$, the finite geometric identity gives
\begin{equation}
\frac{\kappa a^N}{1-(1-\kappa)a^N}
=\frac{a^N}{1+(1-\kappa)h\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}a^j}.
\label{eq:continuous-rate}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Which conjecture is being addressed}

Theorem~\ref{thm:main} is the final-gradient, initial-distance problem
in Conjecture~3, Section~4.1.3, of Taylor--Hendrickx--Glineur
\cite{THG17}. That paper supplies the two types of sharp examples
used below. The normalization matters: a sharp result for
$\norm{\grad f(x_N)}^2/(f(x_0)-f_*)$ does not itself establish the
sharp value of $\norm{\grad f(x_N)}/\norm{x_0-x_*}$.

The relevant prior results have distinct scopes.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=4pt]
\item Taylor--Hendrickx--Glineur \cite{THG18} prove exact same-measure
linear contraction results for proximal gradient. Their Section~4
and Table~1 explicitly leave the mixed final-residual/initial-distance
criterion unknown. The mixed measures proved there run in different
directions.
\item Kim \cite{Kim25} proves the exact final-function-gap rate under
initial-distance normalization and relates it to final-gradient
rates under initial-function-gap normalization.
\item Rotaru--Glineur--Patrinos \cite{RGP26} give exact gradient-norm
rates for all constant steps with initial objective-gap normalization.
This is not a sharp initial-distance result.
\item Chikhi \cite{Chikhi26} proves the optimal constant step, value,
and uniqueness in the smooth-convex final-gradient problem, using a
factorized interpolation certificate. The full curve away from
that optimum is expressly left open there.
\item Jones \cite{Jones26} subsequently establishes the complete
$\kappa=0$ curve, equality trajectories, and related proximal results.
The current repository expressly excludes positive strong convexity
from its established claim. The factorized-certificate approach and
the retained interpolation surplus under scaling are prior ingredients
and are credited accordingly.
\end{itemize}

The close prior exact results in \cite{RGP26,Kim25}, combined with
$f(x_0)-f_*\leq LR^2/2$, give, when $0<\kappa<1$,
$a=1-\kappa h>0$, and $t=a^N$,
the progress factor
\begin{equation}
\frac{\sqrt{\kappa}\,t}{\sqrt{1-(1-\kappa)t^2}},
\quad\hbox{whereas the target factor is}\quad
\frac{\kappa t}{1-(1-\kappa)t}.
\label{eq:prior-comparison}
\end{equation}
The second does not follow from that conversion: the difference of
the relevant squared denominators is
\[
[1-(1-\kappa)t]^2-\kappa[1-(1-\kappa)t^2]
=(1-\kappa)(1-t)^2.
\]
For fixed $0<\kappa<1$ as $t\to0$, the ratio of the target progress
factor to the converted factor tends to $\sqrt{\kappa}$.
The oscillation factor $|1-h|^N$ is the same.

The intended contribution is the full positive-curvature curve.
The established $\kappa=0$ theorem is credited to prior work. The proof below does not use
the convex full-curve theorem as a black box. Basic analytic and
finite-sum infrastructure in the accompanying Lean development is
adapted from the MIT-licensed Jones project; its license and provenance
accompany the source distribution.

A 2023 Glineur lecture presentation \cite{Glineur23} displays the target formula as an
equality under \emph{mixed performance criteria}. This is not treated as
a prior analytic proof: the same presentation distinguishes numerical
PEP output from rigorous proofs, and displays analogous exact-max
formulas whose full proof is expressly unavailable there. The cited
2018 paper's unknown entry and the later primary-source scope statements
are the relevant distinctions. The review is bounded by sources consulted
through 29 October 2026 and is not a worldwide priority claim.

\subsection{Normalization}

Replacing $f$ by $F(z)=(f(x_*+z)-f(x_*))/L$ reduces the proof to
$L=1$, $x_*=0$, $f_*=0$, and curvature $\kappa\in[0,1]$.
Retain notation $f,x_i,g_i$ for normalized data:
\begin{equation}
g_i=\grad f(x_i),\qquad x_{i+1}=x_i-hg_i.
\label{eq:normalized-gd}
\end{equation}
The balanced certificate uses a parameter $\theta$ that may be negative.
The actual function has $\kappa\geq0$; the negative parameter is an
algebraic device.

\section{Interpolation from actual smooth functions}

For data $(x_i,g_i,f_i)$ and $\theta<1$, put
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\Q_\theta(i,j)={}&2(1-\theta)(f_i-f_j)
 -2\ip{g_j-\theta g_i}{x_i-x_j}\\
&-\norm{g_i-g_j}^2-\theta\norm{x_i-x_j}^2.
\end{split}
\label{eq:Q}
\end{equation}
Starred data mean $x_*=g_*=f_*=0$.
The gradient at the actual minimizer is zero because every directional
derivative there vanishes. The smooth descent inequality used below
also follows directly from the Lipschitz hypothesis: for $d=z-x$,
\[
f(z)-f(x)-\ip{\grad f(x)}d
=\int_0^1\ip{\grad f(x+td)-\grad f(x)}d\,dt
\leq\int_0^1t\norm d^2\,dt=\tfrac12\norm d^2.
\]

\begin{lemma}[Necessary interpolation inequality]
\label{lem:interpolation}
If $f$ has a $1$-Lipschitz gradient and satisfies the strong first-order
inequality with parameter $\theta<1$, then
$\Q_\theta(i,j)\geq0$ for its actual values and gradients.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Let $x=x_i$, $y=x_j$, $v=x-y$, $w=\grad f(x)-\grad f(y)$, and set
\[
u=\frac{w-\theta v}{1-\theta},\qquad z=x-u.
\]
Strong first-order support at $y$ and smooth descent at $x$ give
\begin{align*}
f(y)+\ip{\grad f(y)}{z-y}+\frac\theta2\norm{z-y}^2
&\leq f(z),\\
f(z)&\leq f(x)+\ip{\grad f(x)}{z-x}+\frac12\norm{z-x}^2.
\end{align*}
Their difference is nonnegative. Substituting $z-x=-u$ and $z-y=v-u$
shows that it equals
\begin{align*}
&f(x)-f(y)-\ip{\grad f(y)}v-\frac\theta2\norm v^2
 -\ip{w-\theta v}{u}+\frac{1-\theta}{2}\norm u^2\\
&\qquad=
f(x)-f(y)-\ip{\grad f(y)}v-\frac\theta2\norm v^2
 -\frac{\norm{w-\theta v}^2}{2(1-\theta)}.
\end{align*}
Multiplication by $2(1-\theta)>0$ and expansion gives \eqref{eq:Q}.
\end{proof}

Write $\Delta x=x_i-x_j$ and $\Delta g=g_i-g_j$.
\begin{lemma}[Changing curvature and scaling]
\label{lem:changes}
For $\kappa<1$ and arbitrary real $\nu,s$,
\begin{align}
\Q_\nu(i,j)
&=\frac{1-\nu}{1-\kappa}\Q_\kappa(i,j)
 +\frac{\kappa-\nu}{1-\kappa}\norm{\Delta x-\Delta g}^2,
\label{eq:curvature-change}\\
\Q^{(s)}_{s\kappa}(i,j)
&=\frac{s(1-s\kappa)}{1-\kappa}\Q_\kappa(i,j)
 +\frac{s(1-s)}{1-\kappa}\norm{\Delta g-\kappa\Delta x}^2.
\label{eq:scale-change}
\end{align}
Here $\Q^{(s)}$ uses $(x_i,sg_i,sf_i)$.
Thus nonnegative $\Q_\kappa$ imply nonnegative $\Q_\nu$ for
$\nu\leq\kappa<1$, and nonnegative $\Q^{(s)}_{s\kappa}$ for
$0\leq s\leq1$, $0\leq\kappa<1$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
After multiplication by $1-\kappa$, the function-value terms cancel
on subtracting the indicated multiple of $\Q_\kappa$. The remainder
in the first identity is
\[
(\kappa-\nu)(\norm{\Delta x}^2
 -2\ip{\Delta g}{\Delta x}+\norm{\Delta g}^2),
\]
and in the second it is
\[
s(1-s)(\norm{\Delta g}^2
 -2\kappa\ip{\Delta g}{\Delta x}+\kappa^2\norm{\Delta x}^2).
\]
These expansions prove the identities and their sign conclusions.
\end{proof}

For actual normalized convex data, $\Q_0(i,*)\geq0$ gives
$\norm{g_i}^2\leq2f_i$, and
\begin{equation}
\norm{x_i}^2-\norm{g_i}^2
=\Q_0(*,i)+\Q_0(i,*)+\norm{x_i-g_i}^2\geq0.
\label{eq:terminal-bounds}
\end{equation}
These terminal estimates apply even with negative auxiliary curvature.

\section{The balanced scalar construction}

Assume
\begin{equation}
N\geq1,\quad 1<h<2,\quad r=h-1,\quad a>r,\quad
\theta=\frac{1-a}{h}.
\label{eq:balanced-parameters}
\end{equation}
Thus $0<r<1$, $a>0$, and $\theta<1$. No assumption $a\leq1$ is made.
Define
\begin{equation}
S_m=h\sum_{j=1}^{m}a^{-j},\qquad
Y_m=r^{-m},\qquad P_m=\frac{Y_m^2-1}{2}\quad(m\geq0),
\label{eq:scalar-sequences}
\end{equation}
and impose balance:
\begin{equation}
Y_N=1+S_N.
\label{eq:balance}
\end{equation}
Empty sums are zero. Geometric sums give
\begin{align}
aS_{m+1}&=S_m+h,& S_{m+1}-S_m&=ha^{-m-1},\label{eq:S-rec}\\
rY_{m+1}&=Y_m,& r^2P_{m+1}-P_m&=\frac{1-r^2}{2},\label{eq:P-rec}\\
a^{-m}&=1+\theta S_m.&&\label{eq:A-identity}
\end{align}
The last identity follows from
$(a-1)S_m=h(1-a^{-m})$ and $a-1=-\theta h$, including $a=1$.

\subsection{Ratios and their signs}

For $1\leq m\leq N$, put
\begin{equation}
b_m=\frac{S_m}{Y_m+1},\qquad c_m=\frac{S_m}{Y_m-1}.
\label{eq:bc}
\end{equation}
Both are positive. Since
$Y_m-1=(1-r)\sum_{j=1}^{m}r^{-j}$,
\begin{equation}
c_m=\frac{h}{1-r}
 \frac{\sum_{j=1}^{m}a^{-j}}{\sum_{j=1}^{m}r^{-j}}.
\label{eq:c-average}
\end{equation}
The fraction is a weighted average of $(r/a)^j$ with positive
weights $r^{-j}$. Each added value is smaller than every previous one,
so $c_m$ strictly decreases. Balance gives $c_N=1$ and hence
\begin{equation}
S_m>Y_m-1\qquad(1\leq m<N).
\label{eq:prefix-gap}
\end{equation}
For an explicit strict comparison, if $0<p<q$ and
$G_m(t)=\sum_{j=1}^{m}t^j$, then
\[
G_m(p)q^{m+1}-p^{m+1}G_m(q)
=\sum_{j=1}^{m}p^jq^j(q^{m+1-j}-p^{m+1-j})>0.
\]
Using $G_{m+1}(t)=G_m(t)+t^{m+1}$ proves strict decrease of the
ratio; apply $p=a^{-1}$ and $q=r^{-1}$.

\subsection{Four explicit factors}

Use natural boundary indexing:
\begin{equation}
U_0=B_0=0,\quad U_1=1,\quad
U_{m+1}=U_m\frac{1+b_m}{ab_{m+1}-r}\quad(1\leq m<N).
\label{eq:U}
\end{equation}
Define
\begin{equation}
B_m=b_mU_m,\quad D_m=P_m/U_m,\quad V_m=c_mD_m
\quad(1\leq m\leq N),\qquad D_{N+1}=V_{N+1}=0.
\label{eq:BDV}
\end{equation}
Set $D_0=V_0=0$ for terms multiplied by $U_0$ or $B_0$.
Every denominator used in \eqref{eq:U} is positive:
\begin{equation}
ab_{m+1}-r
=\frac{1+S_m-Y_m}{Y_{m+1}+1}>0
\quad(1\leq m<N).
\label{eq:denominator}
\end{equation}
This follows from \eqref{eq:S-rec}, $h=1+r$, and
\eqref{eq:prefix-gap}. The zero denominator $ab_1-r$ is never used.

\begin{lemma}[Factor monotonicity]
\label{lem:factor-signs}
$U_m$ and $B_m$ strictly increase on indices $0,\ldots,N$.
The sequences $D_m,V_m$ are positive on indices $1,\ldots,N$ and
strictly decrease through their terminal value zero at index $N+1$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Positivity of $U_m$ follows from its recursion. The exact difference
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
&(1+b_m)P_m-(ab_{m+1}-r)P_{m+1}\\
&\quad=\frac{h(1-r)Y_m}{2r}
\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m}r^{-j}-\sum_{j=1}^{m}a^{-j}\right)>0
\qquad(1\leq m<N)
\end{split}
\label{eq:cross-gap}
\end{equation}
compares the growth of $U_m$ to that of $P_m$.
To verify it, use $P_mb_m=S_m(Y_m-1)/2$,
$aS_{m+1}=S_m+h$, and $Y_{m+1}=Y_m/r$.
Twice the left side becomes
\[
(Y_m^2-1)+S_m(Y_m-1)
 -(S_m+h)(Y_m/r-1)+r(Y_m^2/r^2-1).
\]
With $h=1+r$ this is $Y_m[h(Y_m-1)-(1-r)S_m]/r$.
Insert the geometric formulas for $Y_m-1$ and $S_m$.
The parenthesis in \eqref{eq:cross-gap} is positive since $r<a$.
Since $P_{m+1}>P_m>0$,
\[
\frac{U_{m+1}}{U_m}
=\frac{1+b_m}{ab_{m+1}-r}
>\frac{P_{m+1}}{P_m}>1.
\]
Thus $U$ increases and $D$ decreases, including the stated boundaries.

The recursion for $U$ gives
\begin{equation}
aB_{m+1}-B_m=rU_{m+1}+U_m\qquad(0\leq m<N).
\label{eq:forward-recurrence}
\end{equation}
At $m=0$ this uses $ab_1=r$. To show that $B$ increases even if
$a>1$, note
\begin{equation}
(a-1)b_m<h:
\label{eq:b-bound}
\end{equation}
indeed $(a-1)S_m=h(1-a^{-m})<h<h(Y_m+1)$.
Multiplying by $U_m>0$ gives
\begin{align*}
aB_m&=B_m+(a-1)B_m<B_m+hU_m\\
&=B_m+U_m+rU_m
 <B_m+U_m+rU_{m+1}=aB_{m+1}.
\end{align*}
Since $a>0$, this proves the claim; $B_1>B_0$ follows from $b_1>0$.
Finally both positive factors in $V_m=c_mD_m$ decrease, so $V$ does
also, including its terminal zero.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Reverse recurrence and product identities}

The second recurrence is
\begin{equation}
V_m-aV_{m+1}=D_m+rD_{m+1}\qquad(1\leq m\leq N).
\label{eq:reverse-recurrence}
\end{equation}
First record
\begin{align}
P_mb_m&=\frac{S_m(Y_m-1)}2,&
P_mc_m&=\frac{S_m(Y_m+1)}2,\label{eq:PbPc}\\
P_m(c_m-b_m)&=S_m,& P_mb_mc_m&=\frac{S_m^2}2.
\label{eq:ratio-products}
\end{align}
For $m<N$ these give
\begin{align}
P_m(c_m-1)(1+b_m)
&=\frac{S_m^2}2+S_m-P_m,\label{eq:tail-left}\\
P_{m+1}(ac_{m+1}+r)(ab_{m+1}-r)
&=\frac{a^2S_{m+1}^2}2-arS_{m+1}-r^2P_{m+1}.
\label{eq:tail-right}
\end{align}
By \eqref{eq:S-rec} and \eqref{eq:P-rec}, the second right side is
\[
\frac{(S_m+h)^2}{2}-r(S_m+h)-P_m-\frac{1-r^2}{2}
=\frac{S_m^2}{2}+S_m-P_m,
\]
because $h-r=1$ and $h^2-2rh-(1-r^2)=0$. Thus
\[
P_m(c_m-1)(1+b_m)
=P_{m+1}(ac_{m+1}+r)(ab_{m+1}-r).
\]
Divide by $U_m(1+b_m)$ and use \eqref{eq:U}:
$(c_m-1)D_m=(ac_{m+1}+r)D_{m+1}$, the desired recurrence.
At $m=N$, use $c_N=1$ and the terminal zero tails.
The needed product identities are
\begin{align}
U_mD_m&=P_m,\label{eq:UD}\\
C_m:=U_mV_m-B_mD_m&=P_m(c_m-b_m)=S_m,\label{eq:C}\\
B_ND_N&=P_Nb_N=S_N^2/2.\label{eq:terminal-BD}
\end{align}
The zero-index cases of the first two identities follow from the
boundary conventions; the last uses $Y_N-1=S_N$.



\section{The exact balanced history identity}

Let data obey \eqref{eq:normalized-gd}. No terminal estimate or sign
assumption on function values is needed for the identity in this section.
Define positive differences
\begin{equation}
\begin{gathered}
u_i=U_{i+1}-U_i,\quad s_i=B_{i+1}-B_i\quad(0\leq i<N),\\
v_j=V_j-V_{j+1},\quad t_j=D_j-D_{j+1}\quad(1\leq j\leq N).
\end{gathered}
\label{eq:factor-differences}
\end{equation}
For $0\leq i<j\leq N$, set
\begin{equation}
\lambda_{ij}=u_iv_j,\qquad \lambda_{ji}=s_it_j,\qquad\lambda_{ii}=0.
\label{eq:multipliers}
\end{equation}
Lemma~\ref{lem:factor-signs} gives $\lambda_{ij}\geq0$. Write
\begin{equation}
d_i=\sum_{j=0}^N(\lambda_{ij}-\lambda_{ji}),\qquad
W=\sum_{i,j=0}^N\lambda_{ij}\Q_\theta(i,j),\qquad S=S_N.
\label{eq:W}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Divergence and cuts}

For each cut $0\leq p<N$, telescoping gives
\begin{align*}
\sum_{i\leq p<j}\lambda_{ij}
&=\left(\sum_{i=0}^pu_i\right)
  \left(\sum_{j=p+1}^Nv_j\right)=U_{p+1}V_{p+1},\\
\sum_{i\leq p<j}\lambda_{ji}
&=\left(\sum_{i=0}^ps_i\right)
  \left(\sum_{j=p+1}^Nt_j\right)=B_{p+1}D_{p+1}.
\end{align*}
Internal edges cancel in the divergence sum, so
$\sum_{i=0}^{p}d_i=C_{p+1}=S_{p+1}$.
Together with $\sum_i d_i=0$, this proves
\begin{equation}
d_i=ha^{-i-1}\quad(i<N),\qquad d_N=-S.
\label{eq:divergence}
\end{equation}

For coefficient bookkeeping let $T_{ij}=\one_{\{j<i\}}$, so
$x_i=x_0-h\sum_kT_{ik}g_k$. Bilinear expansion for arbitrary weights gives
\begin{equation}
W=2(1-\theta)\sum_i d_if_i
 +2\sum_{p,q}M_{pq}\ip{g_p}{g_q}
 -\sum_i I_i\norm{g_i}^2,
\label{eq:general-expansion}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{align}
I_i&=\sum_j(\lambda_{ij}+\lambda_{ji}),\label{eq:incident}\\
M_{pq}&=\lambda_{pq}
 +h\sum_k(\lambda_{kp}+\theta\lambda_{pk})(T_{kq}-T_{pq})\notag\\
&\quad-\frac{\theta h^2}{2}
 \sum_{k,l}\lambda_{kl}(T_{kp}-T_{lp})(T_{kq}-T_{lq}).
\label{eq:M}
\end{align}
The squared gradient difference in \eqref{eq:Q} contributes
$2\lambda_{pq}$ to the ordered inner-product coefficient and $-I_i$
to each diagonal. The linear gradient--position term contributes
the middle sum in \eqref{eq:M}; the position square contributes its
last sum. The common $x_0$ cancels from every $x_i-x_j$.
This accounts for every term of \eqref{eq:Q}.

\subsection{Cancellation of every off-diagonal term}

For $p<q$, the product
$(T_{kp}-T_{lp})(T_{kq}-T_{lq})$ is one exactly when an edge joins an
index at most $p$ to an index greater than $q$, and zero otherwise.
Consequently
\begin{equation}
\sum_{k,l}\lambda_{kl}(T_{kp}-T_{lp})(T_{kq}-T_{lq})
=U_{p+1}V_{q+1}+B_{p+1}D_{q+1}.
\label{eq:double-cut}
\end{equation}
The two linear sums in $M_{pq}+M_{qp}$ are
\begin{align*}
\sum_k(\lambda_{kp}+\theta\lambda_{pk})(T_{kq}-T_{pq})
 &=s_pD_{q+1}+\theta u_pV_{q+1},\\
\sum_k(\lambda_{kq}+\theta\lambda_{qk})(T_{kp}-T_{qp})
 &=-U_{p+1}v_q-\theta B_{p+1}t_q.
\end{align*}
Indeed $T_{pq}=0$, so the first sum only uses $k>q$; $T_{qp}=1$,
so the second only uses $k\leq p$, with a minus sign. Insert the
factorized weights and telescope.

The coefficient of $\ip{g_p}{g_q}$ in $W$, divided by two, is therefore
\begin{align}
M_{pq}+M_{qp}
={}&u_pv_q+s_pt_q
+h(s_pD_{q+1}+\theta u_pV_{q+1}
       -U_{p+1}v_q-\theta B_{p+1}t_q)\notag\\
&-\theta h^2(U_{p+1}V_{q+1}+B_{p+1}D_{q+1})\notag\\
={}&(u_p-hU_{p+1})(v_q+\theta hV_{q+1})
 +(s_p-\theta hB_{p+1})(t_q+hD_{q+1}).
\label{eq:mixed-factorization}
\end{align}
The recurrences, $1-a=\theta h$, and $h=1+r$ give
\begin{align*}
s_p-\theta hB_{p+1}
&=aB_{p+1}-B_p=rU_{p+1}+U_p=hU_{p+1}-u_p,\\
v_q+\theta hV_{q+1}
&=V_q-aV_{q+1}=D_q+rD_{q+1}=t_q+hD_{q+1}.
\end{align*}
Thus the two terms in \eqref{eq:mixed-factorization} cancel.

\subsection{Every diagonal coefficient}

Let $e_i$ be the coefficient of $\norm{g_i}^2$ in $W$.
For $i<N$ abbreviate $U=U_{i+1}$, $B=B_{i+1}$, $D=D_{i+1}$,
$V=V_{i+1}$, and retain $U_i,B_i$ for the preceding prefixes.
The incident sum, divergence, and diagonal expansion are
\begin{align}
I_i&=u_iV+s_iD+B_it_i+U_iv_i,\label{eq:diag-I}\\
d_i&=u_iV-s_iD+B_it_i-U_iv_i,\label{eq:diag-d}\\
e_i&=-I_i+2\theta h u_iV+2h s_iD
       -\theta h^2(UV+BD).\label{eq:diag-e}
\end{align}
Terms $B_0t_0,U_0v_0$ at $i=0$ mean zero. No reverse recurrence
at index zero is assumed. Adding the last two identities yields
\begin{equation}
e_i+d_i
=2r s_iD-2U_iv_i
 +\theta h[(2u_i-hU)V-hBD].
\label{eq:diag-first}
\end{equation}
The forward recurrence gives $s_i=\theta hB+rU+U_i$.
For $i\geq1$, the reverse recurrence gives
$v_i=D_i+rD-\theta hV$; at $i=0$ the substitution is valid after
multiplication by $U_0=0$. Hence
\[
2rs_iD-2U_iv_i
=2r^2UD-2U_iD_i+2r\theta hBD+2\theta hU_iV.
\]
Use $u_i=U-U_i$, \eqref{eq:UD}, and \eqref{eq:C} in
\eqref{eq:diag-first} to obtain
\begin{align}
e_i+d_i
&=2(r^2P_{i+1}-P_i)+\theta h(1-r)(UV-BD)\notag\\
&=(1-r)[h+\theta hS_{i+1}]=(1-r)d_i.
\label{eq:diag-final}
\end{align}
The last step uses $d_i-\theta hS_{i+1}=h$, which follows from
$d_i=S_{i+1}-S_i$ and $aS_{i+1}=S_i+h$.
Thus $e_i=-rd_i$ for every $i<N$. At $i=N$ all interval tails vanish:
\begin{equation}
e_N=-(U_NV_N+B_ND_N)
=-S-2B_ND_N=-S(S+1).
\label{eq:diag-terminal}
\end{equation}
This establishes the full history formula
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
W={}&2(1-\theta)
 \left(\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}ha^{-i-1}f_i-Sf_N\right)\\
&-r\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}ha^{-i-1}\norm{g_i}^2
 -S(S+1)\norm{g_N}^2.
\end{split}
\label{eq:history-formula}
\end{equation}

\subsection{The joint potential with surplus retained}

The star quantity is
\begin{equation}
\Q_\theta(*,i)
=-2(1-\theta)f_i+2\ip{g_i}{x_i}
 -\norm{g_i}^2-\theta\norm{x_i}^2.
\label{eq:starQ}
\end{equation}
Its weighted sum, added to \eqref{eq:history-formula}, cancels all
nonterminal function values. The remaining history term is
\[
\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}ha^{-i-1}
 \left(2\ip{g_i}{x_i}-h\norm{g_i}^2-\theta\norm{x_i}^2\right).
\]
The GD recurrence and $a=1-\theta h$ give exactly
\begin{equation}
ha^{-i-1}
 \left(2\ip{g_i}{x_i}-h\norm{g_i}^2-\theta\norm{x_i}^2\right)
=a^{-i}\norm{x_i}^2-a^{-i-1}\norm{x_{i+1}}^2.
\label{eq:weighted-telescope}
\end{equation}
Summing telescopically proves the central identity.

\begin{proposition}[Balanced joint identity]
\label{prop:balanced}
Under \eqref{eq:balanced-parameters}--\eqref{eq:balance} and the GD
recurrence, the explicitly constructed nonnegative weights satisfy
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\norm{x_0}^2={}&a^{-N}\norm{x_N}^2
 +2(1-\theta)Sf_N+S(S+1)\norm{g_N}^2\\
&+W+\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}ha^{-i-1}\Q_\theta(*,i).
\end{split}
\label{eq:balanced-joint}
\end{equation}
Nonnegative history quantities imply $W\geq0$. If the star quantities
are also nonnegative, their displayed weighted sum is nonnegative.
\end{proposition}

This proof makes no sign assertion about a terminal squared-norm
coefficient in an alternative completion. Such a coefficient can be
negative when $\theta<0$. The joint identity \eqref{eq:balanced-joint}
is the form needed here.

\section{Recovering both branches at every step}

Return to normalized actual functions with $0\leq\kappa<1$,
$N\geq1$, and $0<h<2$. Put $a_0=1-\kappa h$.
All $\Q_\kappa$ are nonnegative by Lemma~\ref{lem:interpolation}.
Whenever $a_0>0$, write
\begin{equation}
S=h\sum_{j=1}^N a_0^{-j},\qquad A=a_0^{-N}=1+\kappa S.
\label{eq:target-SA}
\end{equation}

\subsection{The progress branch}

Assume $a_0>0$ and either $h\leq1$ or
\begin{equation}
(h-1)^N(1+S)\leq1.
\label{eq:progress-condition}
\end{equation}
Let $G=\sum_{j=1}^Na_0^{-j}>0$ and
\[
F(H)=(H-1)^N(1+HG),\qquad H\geq1.
\]
This is continuous, strictly increasing on $(1,\infty)$, and $F(1)=0$.
Moreover
\begin{equation}
F(1+a_0)=a_0^N(1+(1+a_0)G)
\geq a_0^N+1+a_0>1,
\label{eq:F-upper}
\end{equation}
since $G\geq a_0^{-N}$. There is a unique $H\in(1,1+a_0)$ with
$F(H)=1$. The branch condition implies $h\leq H$: this is immediate
for $h\leq1$, and otherwise follows from $F(h)\leq1$.
Since $a_0\leq1$, also $H<2$.

Set $s=h/H\in(0,1]$. The scaled data $(x_i,sg_i,sf_i)$ follow a step
$H$ with parameter $s\kappa$, and
\[
1-(s\kappa)H=a_0,\qquad
S'=H\sum_{j=1}^Na_0^{-j}=S/s,\qquad
(H-1)^{-N}=1+S'.
\]
Their history interpolation quantities are nonnegative by
\eqref{eq:scale-change}. Apply Proposition~\ref{prop:balanced},
retaining its star sum.

Define
\begin{equation}
E_i=\norm{g_i}^2-2\kappa f_i.
\label{eq:E}
\end{equation}
The adjacent identity
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
h(1-\kappa)(E_i-E_{i+1})
={}&a_0\Q_\kappa(i,i+1)+\Q_\kappa(i+1,i)\\
&+(2-h)\norm{g_{i+1}-a_0g_i}^2
\end{split}
\label{eq:E-monotone}
\end{equation}
shows $E_i\geq E_{i+1}$. To verify it, write $g=g_i$, $k=g_{i+1}$
and substitute $x_i-x_{i+1}=hg$ in \eqref{eq:Q}:
\begin{align*}
\Q_\kappa(i,i+1)
={}&2(1-\kappa)(f_i-f_{i+1})
 -2h\ip{k}{g}+2\kappa h\norm g^2
 -\norm{g-k}^2-\kappa h^2\norm g^2,\\
\Q_\kappa(i+1,i)
={}&-2(1-\kappa)(f_i-f_{i+1})
 +2h\norm g^2-2\kappa h\ip{k}{g}
 -\norm{g-k}^2-\kappa h^2\norm g^2.
\end{align*}
In $a_0$ times the first plus the second plus
$(2-h)\norm{k-a_0g}^2$, the coefficients of $f_i-f_{i+1}$,
$\norm g^2$, $\norm k^2$, and $\ip gk$ are respectively
$-2\kappa h(1-\kappa)$, $h(1-\kappa)$, $-h(1-\kappa)$, and zero.
This is \eqref{eq:E-monotone}. Every term on its right is nonnegative,
so in particular $E_i\geq E_N$.

Substitution of star data gives the exact identity
\begin{equation}
\Q^{(s)}_{s\kappa}(*,i)
=s\Q_\kappa(*,i)+s(1-s)E_i.
\label{eq:scaled-star}
\end{equation}
Since $Hs=h$, the retained star remainder satisfies
\begin{align}
\sum_{i<N}Ha_0^{-i-1}\Q^{(s)}_{s\kappa}(*,i)
&=\sum_{i<N}ha_0^{-i-1}\Q_\kappa(*,i)
 +(1-s)\sum_{i<N}ha_0^{-i-1}E_i\notag\\
&\geq(1-s)SE_N.
\label{eq:star-surplus}
\end{align}
The scaled certificate's terminal part is
\[
A\norm{x_N}^2+2(1-s\kappa)Sf_N+S(S+s)\norm{g_N}^2.
\]
Add \eqref{eq:star-surplus} and expand $E_N$. The result is exactly
\begin{equation}
A\norm{x_N}^2+2(1-\kappa)Sf_N
 +S(S+1)\norm{g_N}^2\leq\norm{x_0}^2.
\label{eq:progress-joint}
\end{equation}
There is no limit argument, including at $\kappa=0$.

Using \eqref{eq:terminal-bounds}, $A>0$, $S>0$, and $A=1+\kappa S$,
the left side is at least
\[
\bigl(A+(1-\kappa)S+S(S+1)\bigr)\norm{g_N}^2
=(1+S)^2\norm{g_N}^2.
\]
Hence
\begin{equation}
\norm{g_N}\leq\frac{\norm{x_0}}{1+S}.
\label{eq:progress-bound}
\end{equation}
For $\kappa=0$, $S=Nh$. For $\kappa>0$, $A=1+\kappa S$ gives
\[
\frac1{1+S}
=\frac{\kappa a_0^N}{1-(1-\kappa)a_0^N}=p_N(\kappa,h).
\]
Thus this is the claimed progress estimate.

\subsection{The oscillation branch and negative auxiliary curvature}

Assume $1<h<2$, let $r=h-1$, and put $T=r^{-N}-1>0$.
The function
\[
\mathcal S(b)=h\sum_{j=1}^Nb^{-j},\qquad b>0,
\]
is continuous, strictly decreasing, and tends to zero as $b\to\infty$.
At $b=r$,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal S(r)=\frac{h}{1-r}(r^{-N}-1)>T.
\label{eq:upper-IVT}
\end{equation}
There is therefore a unique $b>r$ with $\mathcal S(b)=T$.

Suppose either $a_0\leq0$, or
\begin{equation}
a_0>0,\qquad \mathcal S(a_0)\geq r^{-N}-1.
\label{eq:upper-condition}
\end{equation}
Then $b\geq a_0$: the first case is immediate and the second follows
from strict decrease of $\mathcal S$. Set
\[
\nu=\frac{1-b}{h}\leq\kappa.
\]
Also $\nu<1$ because $b>0$ and $h>1$. By
\eqref{eq:curvature-change}, all history and star quantities
$\Q_\nu$ are nonnegative. The balanced identity with $a=b$, $S=T$
therefore gives
\begin{equation}
b^{-N}\norm{x_N}^2+2(1-\nu)Sf_N
 +S(S+1)\norm{g_N}^2\leq\norm{x_0}^2.
\label{eq:upper-joint}
\end{equation}
Here $b^{-N}=1+\nu S>0$ even if $\nu<0$.
The terminal bounds belong to the actual convex function, and all
coefficients multiplying them are nonnegative. The left side of
\eqref{eq:upper-joint} is thus at least
\[
\bigl(b^{-N}+(1-\nu)S+S(S+1)\bigr)\norm{g_N}^2
=(1+S)^2\norm{g_N}^2=r^{-2N}\norm{g_N}^2.
\]
It follows that
\begin{equation}
\norm{g_N}\leq(h-1)^N\norm{x_0}.
\label{eq:upper-bound}
\end{equation}

The reductions cover every $0<h<2$. For $h\leq1$, $a_0>0$ and the
progress condition applies. For $h>1$, $a_0>0$, compare
$(h-1)^N(1+\mathcal S(a_0))$ with one: values at most one give the
progress branch and values at least one give
\eqref{eq:upper-condition}. If $a_0\leq0$, the oscillation branch
applies directly. At equality either proof is valid.

\section{Endpoints and equal curvature}

For $N=0$ or $h=0$, Lipschitz continuity and $\grad f(x_*)=0$ give
$\norm{\grad f(x_N)}\leq LR$. The factor \eqref{eq:rate} equals one.

For $h=2$, normalized convex interpolation gives
$\norm{g_i}^2\leq\ip{g_i}{x_i}$ by adding the two star inequalities.
More generally, when $0\leq h\leq2$,
\begin{align*}
\norm{x_i-hg_i}^2
&=\norm{x_i}^2-2h\ip{g_i}{x_i}+h^2\norm{g_i}^2\\
&\leq\norm{x_i}^2-h(2-h)\norm{g_i}^2
\leq\norm{x_i}^2.
\end{align*}
Thus $\norm{g_N}\leq\norm{x_N}\leq\norm{x_0}$ at $h=2$.
Here $|1-h|^N=1$ and $p_N(\kappa,h)\leq1$, so the exact maximum
factor is one.

When $\mu=L$, adding the two strong first-order inequalities yields
\[
L\norm{x-x_*}^2\leq\ip{\grad f(x)}{x-x_*},
\qquad \norm{\grad f(x)}\leq L\norm{x-x_*}.
\]
Consequently
\begin{align*}
\norm{\grad f(x)-L(x-x_*)}^2
&=\norm{\grad f(x)}^2-2L\ip{\grad f(x)}{x-x_*}
  +L^2\norm{x-x_*}^2\leq0.
\end{align*}
The centered gradient is exactly $L(x-x_*)$. Therefore
\[
x_i-x_*=(1-h)^i(x_0-x_*),\qquad
\norm{\grad f(x_N)}=L|1-h|^N R.
\]
Since $p_N(1,h)=(1-h)^N$, this is exactly \eqref{eq:rate}.
Together with the two reductions and normalization, this proves the
bound in Theorem~\ref{thm:main}.



\section{Sharpness of both branches}

The two types of examples below already appear in the original
conjecture \cite{THG17}. Their trajectories are checked below to make the
exactness of the bound explicit.

The normalized quadratic
\begin{equation}
f_{\rm quad}(x)=x^2/2,\qquad x_0=1,
\label{eq:quadratic-example}
\end{equation}
belongs to every class $0\leq\kappa\leq1$ and has
$x_i=(1-h)^i$. Its final gradient norm is $|1-h|^N$, attaining the
maximum whenever the oscillation branch dominates.

Suppose $N\geq1$, $h>0$, $0\leq\kappa<1$, and the progress branch
dominates. Then $a=1-\kappa h>0$. Indeed, if $a\leq0$, then $h>1$,
$|a|=\kappa h-1<h-1$, and
$p_N(\kappa,h)\leq|a|^N<(h-1)^N$. The first inequality is immediate
for $a^N\leq0$; otherwise
$\kappa/[1-(1-\kappa)a^N]\leq1$.

Define $S_m=h\sum_{j=1}^m a^{-j}$ and $\tau=1/(1+S_N)$.
The strongly convex Huber spline is
\begin{equation}
f_\tau(x)=
\begin{cases}
\frac12x^2,&|x|\leq\tau,\\[3pt]
\frac\kappa2x^2+(1-\kappa)\tau|x|
 -\frac{1-\kappa}{2}\tau^2,&|x|\geq\tau.
\end{cases}
\label{eq:huber-example}
\end{equation}
Its derivative is continuous at the joining points and satisfies
\begin{equation}
f_\tau'(x)=\kappa x+
 (1-\kappa)\operatorname{clip}_{[-\tau,\tau]}(x).
\label{eq:clipped-gradient}
\end{equation}
The clipped function is nondecreasing and $1$-Lipschitz. Thus the
derivative is $1$-Lipschitz and
$f_\tau(x)-\kappa x^2/2$ is convex. The function is nonnegative and
zero is a minimizer.

For $0\leq i\leq N$, set
\begin{equation}
x_i=\tau(1+S_{N-i}).
\label{eq:sharp-trajectory}
\end{equation}
Then $x_0=1$, $x_N=\tau$, and $x_i>\tau$ for $i<N$.
Using $1+\kappa S_m=a^{-m}$,
\[
f_\tau'(x_i)=\kappa x_i+(1-\kappa)\tau
=\tau a^{-(N-i)}\qquad(0\leq i\leq N).
\]
The increment formula for $S_m$ gives
\[
x_i-x_{i+1}
=\tau(S_{N-i}-S_{N-i-1})
=h\tau a^{-(N-i)}=h f_\tau'(x_i).
\]
Thus \eqref{eq:sharp-trajectory} is the actual GD trajectory and
$f_\tau'(x_N)=\tau=p_N(\kappa,h)$, including $\kappa=0$.

The cases $N=0$, $h=0$, or $\kappa=1$ are attained by the quadratic.
For general $L$ and $R>0$, rescale a normalized witness by
\[
\widetilde f(x)=LR^2 f(x/R),\qquad \widetilde x_0=R.
\]
Its smoothness constant is $L$, its strong-convexity parameter is
$\mu=L\kappa$, and its final gradient is $LR$ times the normalized one.
This proves the sharpness assertion of Theorem~\ref{thm:main}.
